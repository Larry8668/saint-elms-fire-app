# The Sun's Annual Path and the Ecliptic

## Overview
The Sun rises in the east, sets in the west, and gradually changes its position against the background stars over the year. The path it traces on the celestial sphere is the **ecliptic** — a great circle tilted at about $23.4°$ to the celestial equator. The tilt of the ecliptic is the cause of the seasons, the origin of the zodiac, and the basis of the Sun-centred coordinate system used in planetary astronomy. This lesson develops the geometry of the ecliptic and its consequences.

## Learning Path
- What you should already know: the celestial sphere, Earth's rotation and orbit, the celestial equator.
- What this lesson adds: the ecliptic, the obliquity, the seasons, and the zodiac.
- What it unlocks: planetary motion in *Astrophysics II*, sidereal vs. synodic periods, and the precession of the equinoxes (next lesson).

## Core Explanation
**The ecliptic.** As the Earth orbits the Sun, the Sun appears to move against the background of the fixed stars. Over a year, the Sun traces a great circle on the celestial sphere — the **ecliptic**. The ecliptic is the projection of the Earth's orbital plane onto the celestial sphere.

**The obliquity.** The Earth's rotation axis is tilted with respect to its orbital plane. The angle is the **obliquity of the ecliptic**, $\varepsilon \approx 23.4°$. This tilt means the ecliptic and the celestial equator are inclined at $23.4°$ to each other. The two circles intersect at two points — the **equinoxes**.

**Equinoxes and solstices.** The two intersection points are the **vernal (March) equinox** and the **autumnal (September) equinox**. At these points, the Sun crosses the celestial equator, and day and night are approximately equal in length everywhere on Earth.

Halfway between the equinoxes, the Sun reaches its maximum displacement north (June solstice) or south (December solstice). At the June solstice, the Sun is over the Tropic of Cancer ($23.4°\text{ N}$); at the December solstice, over the Tropic of Capricorn ($23.4°\text{ S}$). The word "solstice" comes from the Latin for "Sun standing still" — the Sun's declination reaches a maximum and reverses.

**Seasons.** The seasons are caused by the obliquity, not by the Earth–Sun distance. In June, the Northern Hemisphere tilts toward the Sun, so the Sun is higher in the sky and daylight is longer; both effects deliver more energy per unit area. In December, the opposite. The Earth–Sun distance varies by only about 3.4% over the year, far too small to cause the seasons by itself.

**The zodiac.** The constellations along the ecliptic form the **zodiac**, traditionally 12 constellations: Aries, Taurus, Gemini, Cancer, Leo, Virgo, Libra, Scorpio, Sagittarius, Capricornus, Aquarius, Pisces. (A 13th, Ophiuchus, lies on the ecliptic but is not in the traditional zodiac.) The Sun spends about a month in each.

**Ecliptic coordinates.** In the ecliptic coordinate system, an object's position is given by **ecliptic longitude** $\lambda$ (measured eastward along the ecliptic from the vernal equinox) and **ecliptic latitude** $\beta$ (measured perpendicular to the ecliptic). The Sun, by definition, has $\beta = 0$ always, and $\lambda$ increases by about $1°$ per day.

**Length of the day.** The interval between successive meridian transits of the Sun is the **solar day** (~24 hours). The interval between successive meridian transits of a fixed star is the **sidereal day** (~23h 56m 4s). The solar day is longer because the Earth has to rotate a little extra to "catch up" with the Sun, which has moved about $1°$ east on the ecliptic during one rotation.

## Key Ideas
- The ecliptic is the Sun's apparent annual path on the celestial sphere.
- The obliquity of the ecliptic is $\varepsilon \approx 23.4°$.
- The equinoxes are where the ecliptic meets the celestial equator; the solstices are the extremes.
- The seasons are caused by the obliquity, not by Earth–Sun distance.
- The zodiac is the band of constellations along the ecliptic.

## Worked Examples
**Example 1 — Noon altitude of the Sun at the equinox.** At latitude $\phi = 30°\text{ N}$ on the day of an equinox, the Sun's declination is $0°$. The altitude at upper culmination is $90° - \phi = 60°$.

**Example 2 — Noon altitude at the June solstice.** On June 21, the Sun's declination is $\delta = +\varepsilon = +23.4°$. At latitude $30°\text{ N}$, the noon altitude is $90° - \phi + \delta = 90° - 30° + 23.4° = 83.4°$. The Sun is nearly overhead.

**Example 3 — Day length at the equinox.** The day length depends on the hour angle at sunrise/sunset, which depends on the Sun's declination. At the equinox, $\delta = 0$ and the day length is $12$ hours everywhere. As $\delta$ moves north, days lengthen in the Northern Hemisphere and shorten in the Southern. The Tropic of Cancer ($\phi = +23.4°$) is the latitude where, at the June solstice, the Sun is exactly at the zenith at noon.

## Common Misconceptions
- **"The seasons are caused by the Earth being closer to the Sun in summer."** No. The Earth is closest to the Sun in early January (perihelion), when the Northern Hemisphere is in winter. The seasons are caused by the tilt, not the distance.
- **"The Sun is in Aries in March."** It used to be — when the zodiac was defined ~2000 years ago, the vernal equinox was in Aries. Due to precession, it has moved into Pisces and is approaching Aquarius.
- **"The zodiac has always had 12 signs."** The Babylonians had varying numbers at different times. The IAU has fixed the constellations, and Ophiuchus is the 13th on the ecliptic.
- **"The day is exactly 24 hours."** The solar day varies through the year because the Earth's orbit is elliptical and the ecliptic is tilted. Mean solar day is 24 hours; apparent solar day varies by up to ~30 seconds.

## Connections
The obliquity is one of the *Milankovitch cycles* that drive long-term climate variation (along with eccentricity and precession). The same obliquity sets the latitude of the Arctic and Antarctic circles ($90° - \varepsilon$). The ecliptic plane is the natural reference for the Solar System — used for planetary orbits in *Astrophysics II* and for spacecraft trajectories.

## Takeaway
- The ecliptic is the Sun's apparent annual path; the obliquity is its tilt to the celestial equator.
- The equinoxes are the intersections of the ecliptic and the celestial equator.
- The solstices are the extremes of the Sun's declination; they mark the start of summer and winter.
- Seasons are caused by the obliquity, not by Earth–Sun distance.
- The zodiac is the band of constellations along the ecliptic; the Sun moves about $1°$ per day along it.
