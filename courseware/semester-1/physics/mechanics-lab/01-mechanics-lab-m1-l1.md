# Laboratory Orientation, Safety and Measurement

## Overview

The first lesson of the Mechanics Lab is the orientation. The lesson covers the safety practices of the laboratory, the use of personal protective equipment, the SI units and the standard prefixes, and the use of the standard instruments for length measurement: the metre rule, the vernier caliper, and the micrometer screw gauge. The lesson is the foundation of every subsequent lab course in the programme; without the safety practices, the measurement skills, and the use of the standard instruments, the experimental work is unreliable. The lesson closes with a discussion of error and uncertainty, the subject of the next lesson.

## Learning Path

- **What you should already know**: the SI units and the standard prefixes (from the Bridge Course); the concept of significant figures.
- **What this lesson adds**: the safety practices of the laboratory; the use of PPE; the use of the vernier caliper and the micrometer; the concepts of random and systematic error.
- **What later lessons this will unlock**: error analysis in Lesson m1-l2; force and equilibrium in Lesson m1-l3; friction in Lesson m2-l1; projectile and circular motion in Lesson m2-l2; rotational dynamics in Lesson m2-l3; the report writing in Lesson m3-1.

## Core Explanation

### Laboratory safety

The laboratory is the most safety-critical part of your education. The hazards are real and the consequences of carelessness are severe. The golden rule: **there are no small accidents in the lab**.

The standard safety practices:

- **Read the lab manual** before coming to the lab. Understand the experiment, the apparatus, and the safety considerations.
- **Wear the required PPE**: lab coat, safety goggles, closed-toe shoes. Tie back long hair; secure loose clothing and dangling jewellery.
- **No eating, drinking, smoking, or chewing gum** in the lab.
- **No running or roughhousing** in the lab.
- **No unauthorised experiments**. Do only the experiment in the manual. Do not improvise.
- **Know the location** of the fire extinguisher, the eyewash, the safety shower, the first-aid kit, the emergency exit, and the emergency stop.
- **Report any incident** (spill, breakage, near miss, injury) to the lab instructor immediately, even if it seems minor.
- **Clean up your workspace** before leaving the lab. Wash your hands thoroughly.

### Personal protective equipment (PPE)

The standard PPE for a physics lab:

- **Lab coat**: protects clothing from spills and from minor impacts. Buttoned up.
- **Safety goggles**: protect the eyes from projectiles, broken glass, and chemical splashes. Worn whenever there is a risk of impact or splash.
- **Gloves**: protect the hands from chemicals and from contamination. Not a substitute for hand washing.
- **Closed-toe shoes**: protect the feet from spills and from dropped objects. No sandals, no open heels.

For specific experiments, additional PPE may be required: ear protection for loud experiments, lead aprons for X-ray experiments, respiratory protection for dusty environments.

### The SI system

The **International System of Units (SI)** defines seven base units:

| Quantity | Unit | Symbol |
|---|---|---|
| Length | metre | m |
| Mass | kilogram | kg |
| Time | second | s |
| Electric current | ampere | A |
| Temperature | kelvin | K |
| Amount of substance | mole | mol |
| Luminous intensity | candela | cd |

All other units in physics (newton, joule, watt, pascal, etc.) are derived from these base units. The seven base units are the foundation of the SI; the derived units are products of powers of the base units.

### Prefixes

SI prefixes scale a unit by powers of ten:

- $10^{12}$ tera (T), $10^9$ giga (G), $10^6$ mega (M), $10^3$ kilo (k)
- $10^0$ (no prefix)
- $10^{-3}$ milli (m), $10^{-6}$ micro ($\mu$), $10^{-9}$ nano (n), $10^{-12}$ pico (p)

A $1.5$ GHz processor has a clock at $1.5 \times 10^9$ Hz. A $50$ nm transistor has a feature size of $50 \times 10^{-9}$ m. Prefixes simplify the writing of very large and very small numbers.

### Conversion between unit systems

Conversions between SI and other unit systems:

- Length: $1$ inch $= 2.54$ cm; $1$ foot $= 0.3048$ m; $1$ mile $= 1.609$ km.
- Mass: $1$ pound $= 0.4536$ kg; $1$ ounce $= 28.35$ g.
- Time: $1$ hour $= 3600$ s; $1$ day $= 86400$ s; $1$ year $\approx 3.156 \times 10^7$ s.
- Force: $1$ dyne $= 10^{-5}$ N; $1$ pound-force $= 4.448$ N.
- Energy: $1$ erg $= 10^{-7}$ J; $1$ calorie $= 4.184$ J; $1$ electron-volt $= 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ J.

The conversion is a multiplication by a dimensionless ratio; both sides have the same physical dimension, only the unit changes.

### Significant figures

The number of **significant figures** in a quoted value reflects its precision. The length $1.234$ m has four significant figures; $1.2340$ m has five; $0.0012$ m has two. The trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant; the leading zeros are not.

When multiplying or dividing, the result has the number of significant figures of the least precise input. $1.23 \times 4.5 = 5.535$, but the answer should be reported as $5.5$ (two significant figures, the precision of $4.5$).

When adding or subtracting, the result has the number of decimal places of the least precise input. $1.234 + 0.05 = 1.284$, but the second input has only two decimal places, so the answer is $1.28$.

### The metre rule

A **metre rule** is a ruler of length $1$ m, graduated in millimetres. The smallest division is $1$ mm; the precision is typically $\pm 0.5$ mm (limited by the ability to align the zero of the rule with one end of the object and to read the position of the other end).

The metre rule is used for measurements of length from a few cm to about $1$ m, with a precision of about $\pm 0.5$ mm. For more precise measurements, use a vernier caliper or a micrometer screw gauge.

### The vernier caliper

A **vernier caliper** has a main scale (graduated in cm and mm) and a sliding vernier scale (graduated in $0.1$ mm or $0.05$ mm divisions). The vernier scale slides along the main scale; the reading is the alignment of the vernier with the main scale.

To read a vernier caliper:

1. Read the main scale at the position of the zero of the vernier scale. This gives the millimetres (and the half-mm if the zero is between two marks).
2. Find the vernier division that aligns most closely with a main-scale division. The number of that division (counted from the zero) is the decimal part of the reading (in units of the vernier precision).

For a vernier with $10$ divisions spanning $9$ mm, the precision is $0.1$ mm. For a vernier with $20$ divisions spanning $19$ mm, the precision is $0.05$ mm.

The vernier caliper is used for measurements of length up to about $15$ cm (or $30$ cm for larger calipers), with a precision of $0.1$ mm or $0.05$ mm.

### The micrometer screw gauge

A **micrometer screw gauge** has a spindle that advances by a known amount per turn (typically $0.5$ mm per turn). The thimble is graduated with $50$ divisions, so each division is $0.01$ mm. The reading is the sum of the main scale (mm) and the thimble scale (0.01 mm).

To read a micrometer:

1. Read the main scale at the edge of the thimble. This gives the mm (and the half-mm if visible).
2. Read the thimble scale at the reference line. This gives the 0.01 mm.
3. Add the two readings.

The micrometer is used for measurements of length up to about $25$ mm, with a precision of $0.01$ mm. The zero of the micrometer must be checked (and adjusted if necessary) by closing the jaws and reading the zero error.

### Error and uncertainty

Every measurement has an **uncertainty** — a range within which the true value is believed to lie. The uncertainty arises from the precision of the instrument, the skill of the experimenter, and the variability of the system being measured.

There are two types of error:

- **Random error**: the variability in repeated measurements. The standard deviation of the measurements estimates the random error. The mean of many measurements is more precise than a single measurement, by a factor of $1/\sqrt{N}$ (for $N$ independent measurements).
- **Systematic error**: a bias in the measurement, due to a mis-calibrated instrument, a flawed technique, or a constant environmental factor. The systematic error is reduced by calibration, by careful technique, and by control of the environment.

The total uncertainty is the combination of the random and systematic errors. The result is reported as $x \pm \delta x$, where $x$ is the best estimate and $\delta x$ is the uncertainty. The relative uncertainty is $\delta x / x$.

### Common pitfalls in measurement

- **Parallax**: reading a scale from an angle. The reading is shifted by the angle; always read perpendicular to the scale.
- **Zero error**: the instrument reads a non-zero value when the measured quantity is zero. Correct by subtracting the zero error from all readings.
- **Calibration**: the instrument may be mis-calibrated. Calibrate against a known standard.
- **Wear and damage**: instruments can wear out or be damaged. Check the instrument before use.
- **Hysteresis**: some instruments (e.g. springs, magnets) show different readings on the way up and on the way down. Take the average, or use the same direction throughout.
- **Temperature**: many instruments are temperature-sensitive. Allow the instrument to come to thermal equilibrium with the environment before use.

Compute the area of a rectangle with length $1.23$ m and width $0.45$ m.

**Solution.** Area = $1.23 \times 0.45 = 0.5535$ m$^2$. The width has two significant figures, so the area should be reported as $0.55$ m$^2$ (two significant figures).

### Common pitfalls in the lab

- **Not reading the manual first**: come prepared, know what you're doing.
- **Not wearing PPE**: the PPE is mandatory, not optional.
- **Not recording data properly**: record data in a lab notebook with units, uncertainties, and dates. A loose sheet of paper is not a record.
- **Not checking zero errors**: always check the zero before starting.
- **Not repeating measurements**: a single measurement is not enough. Take at least three.
- **Not considering systematic errors**: think about what could be biasing the measurement.
- **Not writing up the lab properly**: a good lab report is part of the experiment. The data is only half the work.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Unit conversion

Convert $60$ miles per hour to metres per second.

**Solution.** $60$ miles/hour $= 60 \times 1.609$ km/hour $= 96.54$ km/hour $= 96.54 \times 1000 / 3600$ m/s $\approx 26.8$ m/s.

### Example 2 — Significant figures

Compute the volume of a sphere with radius $1.234$ m.

**Solution.** $V = (4/3) \pi r^3 = (4/3) \pi (1.234)^3 = 7.871$ m$^3$. The radius has four significant figures, so the volume has four: $7.871$ m$^3$.

### Example 3 — Uncertainty of a mean

Five measurements of a length give $12.34, 12.36, 12.33, 12.35, 12.34$ cm. Find the mean and the standard deviation.

**Solution.** Mean $= (12.34 + 12.36 + 12.33 + 12.35 + 12.34) / 5 = 61.72 / 5 = 12.344$ cm. Deviations from the mean: $0.004, 0.016, -0.014, 0.006, -0.004$ cm. Squared deviations: $0.000016, 0.000256, 0.000196, 0.000036, 0.000016$ (cm$^2$). Sum $= 0.00052$ cm$^2$. Sample variance $= 0.00052 / 4 = 0.00013$ cm$^2$. Standard deviation $= \sqrt{0.00013} = 0.0114$ cm. Standard error of the mean $= 0.0114 / \sqrt{5} = 0.0051$ cm. The result is $12.344 \pm 0.005$ cm.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"PPE is optional."** No, it is mandatory. The instructor's word is final.
- **"A single measurement is enough."** No, a single measurement is only one data point. Repeat at least three times.
- **"Significant figures are about being precise."** They are about honestly reporting precision. Reporting too many figures overstates your knowledge; reporting too few throws away information.
- **"The error is the difference between my measurement and the textbook value."** No, that is the accuracy, not the precision. The error is the random and systematic uncertainty of the measurement itself.
- **"Zero error is the same as random error."** No, zero error is a systematic error. Correct by subtracting the zero reading from all measurements.
