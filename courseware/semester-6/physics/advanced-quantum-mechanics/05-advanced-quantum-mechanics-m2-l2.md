# Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients

## Overview

Real systems carry several angular momenta: an electron has orbital and spin angular momentum, helium has two electron spins, and hydrogen couples electron and proton spins. When interactions such as $\hat{\mathbf L}\cdot\hat{\mathbf S}$ or $\hat{\mathbf S}_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf S}_2$ are present, the individual projections $m_1$ and $m_2$ are no longer good quantum numbers, but the total angular momentum $\hat{\mathbf J} = \hat{\mathbf J}_1 + \hat{\mathbf J}_2$ and its projection are. This lesson changes from the product (uncoupled) basis to the total (coupled) basis. The allowed values of $j$ follow from a counting argument, and the expansion coefficients, the Clebsch–Gordan coefficients, are generated by the ladder operators of Lesson m2-l1 together with orthogonality. Two cases are worked in full: two spin-1/2 particles, giving the triplet and singlet, and orbital angular momentum $l$ coupled to spin $\tfrac12$, the basis of fine structure.

## Learning Path

- **What you should already know**: the angular-momentum algebra, ladder coefficients and Pauli matrices (Lesson m2-l1); the vector model of $\mathbf J = \mathbf L + \mathbf S$ and term symbols (atomic-and-molecular-physics Lesson m1-l2).
- **What this lesson adds**: tensor-product spaces, coupled and uncoupled bases, the triangle rule, the construction of Clebsch–Gordan coefficients, the spin-1/2 triplet and singlet, the $l\otimes\tfrac12$ states, and the operator identities for $\hat{\mathbf S}_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf S}_2$ and $\hat{\mathbf L}\cdot\hat{\mathbf S}$.
- **What later lessons this will unlock**: exchange symmetry of the singlet and triplet in helium (Lesson m2-l3), the anomalous Zeeman effect and Landé $g$-factor (Lesson m3-l1), and dipole selection rules from coupling with the vector operator $\hat{\mathbf r}$ (Lesson m3-l2).

## Core Explanation

### Tensor products and the two bases

The state space of two angular momenta is the tensor product of their spaces, of dimension $(2j_1 + 1)(2j_2 + 1)$. The **uncoupled basis** $|j_1 m_1\rangle|j_2 m_2\rangle \equiv |m_1 m_2\rangle$ diagonalises $\{\hat J_1^2, \hat J_{1z}, \hat J_2^2, \hat J_{2z}\}$. Because operators of different particles commute, $\hat{\mathbf J} = \hat{\mathbf J}_1 + \hat{\mathbf J}_2$ obeys the angular-momentum algebra, so it has eigenstates $|j m\rangle$ with all the properties of Lesson m2-l1. The **coupled basis** $|j_1 j_2; j m\rangle$ diagonalises $\{\hat J_1^2, \hat J_2^2, \hat J^2, \hat J_z\}$. The two sets are each complete but incompatible, because $\hat J^2 = \hat J_1^2 + \hat J_2^2 + 2\hat{\mathbf J}_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf J}_2$ contains $\hat J_{1x}\hat J_{2x} + \hat J_{1y}\hat J_{2y}$, which does not commute with $\hat J_{1z}$.

The two bases are related by a unitary transformation whose matrix elements are the **Clebsch–Gordan coefficients**:

$$|j m\rangle = \sum_{m_1 + m_2 = m}\langle m_1 m_2|j m\rangle\,|m_1 m_2\rangle.$$

Since $\hat J_z = \hat J_{1z} + \hat J_{2z}$, only terms with $m_1 + m_2 = m$ contribute. In the Condon–Shortley convention all coefficients are real, and $\langle j_1, j_1; j_2, j - j_1|j j\rangle > 0$.

### Allowed values of $j$

The largest possible $m$ is $j_1 + j_2$, reached by one product state, so $j_{\max} = j_1 + j_2$. The value $m = j_1 + j_2 - 1$ is reached by two product states; one combination belongs to $j_{\max}$, the other must start a new multiplet with $j = j_1 + j_2 - 1$. Continuing, each step down adds one new multiplet until the number of product states with given $m$ stops growing, at $m = |j_1 - j_2|$. Hence the **triangle rule**

$$j = |j_1 - j_2|,\ |j_1 - j_2| + 1,\ \ldots,\ j_1 + j_2,$$

and the dimension checks: $\sum_j(2j + 1) = (2j_1 + 1)(2j_2 + 1)$.

### Constructing the coefficients

The recipe is always the same. (i) The top state $|j_{\max}, j_{\max}\rangle = |j_1\rangle|j_2\rangle$ is unique. (ii) Apply $\hat J_- = \hat J_{1-} + \hat J_{2-}$ to both sides, using $\hat J_-|j,m\rangle = \hbar\sqrt{j(j+1) - m(m-1)}\,|j,m-1\rangle$ on each, to generate the whole $j_{\max}$ multiplet. (iii) The top state of the next multiplet, $|j_{\max} - 1, j_{\max} - 1\rangle$, is the combination of the same product states orthogonal to $|j_{\max}, j_{\max} - 1\rangle$, with the sign fixed by convention. (iv) Lower again, and repeat.

### Two spin-1/2 particles

Here $j_1 = j_2 = \tfrac12$, so $S = 1$ or $0$, and $3 + 1 = 4 = 2\times2$. Start with $|1,1\rangle = |{\uparrow\uparrow}\rangle$. Lowering, $\hat S_-|1,1\rangle = \hbar\sqrt2\,|1,0\rangle$ and $(\hat S_{1-} + \hat S_{2-})|{\uparrow\uparrow}\rangle = \hbar(|{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle + |{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle)$, so

$$|1,1\rangle = |{\uparrow\uparrow}\rangle, \qquad |1,0\rangle = \frac{|{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle + |{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle}{\sqrt2}, \qquad |1,-1\rangle = |{\downarrow\downarrow}\rangle,$$

the **triplet**. The orthogonal combination is the **singlet**

$$|0,0\rangle = \frac{|{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle - |{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle}{\sqrt2}.$$

The triplet is symmetric and the singlet antisymmetric under exchange of the two spins, a fact central to Lesson m2-l3. Squaring $\hat{\mathbf S} = \hat{\mathbf S}_1 + \hat{\mathbf S}_2$ gives the operator identity

$$\hat{\mathbf S}_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf S}_2 = \tfrac12\left(\hat S^2 - \hat S_1^2 - \hat S_2^2\right) = \tfrac{\hbar^2}{2}\left[S(S+1) - \tfrac32\right] = \begin{cases}+\tfrac14\hbar^2 & S = 1\\ -\tfrac34\hbar^2 & S = 0.\end{cases}$$

An interaction $A\,\hat{\mathbf S}_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf S}_2/\hbar^2$ therefore splits triplet from singlet by exactly $A$. In hydrogen this hyperfine interaction between electron and proton spins gives a splitting of $5.87\ \mu$eV, the 21 cm line.

### Orbital angular momentum $l$ with spin $\tfrac12$

Here $j = l \pm \tfrac12$ for $l \geq 1$, with $(2l + 2) + 2l = 2(2l + 1)$ states. Work out $l = 1$ first. The top state is $|\tfrac32,\tfrac32\rangle = |1\rangle|{\uparrow}\rangle$, writing $|m_l\rangle$ for the orbital factor. Lowering, $\hat J_-|\tfrac32,\tfrac32\rangle = \hbar\sqrt3\,|\tfrac32,\tfrac12\rangle$, while $\hat L_-|1\rangle = \hbar\sqrt2\,|0\rangle$ and $\hat S_-|{\uparrow}\rangle = \hbar|{\downarrow}\rangle$. Hence

$$|\tfrac32,\tfrac12\rangle = \sqrt{\tfrac23}\,|0\rangle|{\uparrow}\rangle + \sqrt{\tfrac13}\,|1\rangle|{\downarrow}\rangle, \qquad |\tfrac12,\tfrac12\rangle = -\sqrt{\tfrac13}\,|0\rangle|{\uparrow}\rangle + \sqrt{\tfrac23}\,|1\rangle|{\downarrow}\rangle,$$

the second fixed by orthogonality and by the sign convention (positive coefficient for $m_l = l$). Repeating for general $l$ gives the standard result

$$|l\pm\tfrac12, m\rangle = \pm\sqrt{\frac{l \pm m + \tfrac12}{2l + 1}}\;|m - \tfrac12\rangle|{\uparrow}\rangle + \sqrt{\frac{l \mp m + \tfrac12}{2l + 1}}\;|m + \tfrac12\rangle|{\downarrow}\rangle,$$

which reproduces the $l = 1$ states above. The full table for $1\otimes\tfrac12$ is

| $\lvert j, m\rangle$ | $\lvert 1\rangle\lvert\uparrow\rangle$ | $\lvert 0\rangle\lvert\uparrow\rangle$ | $\lvert 1\rangle\lvert\downarrow\rangle$ | $\lvert -1\rangle\lvert\uparrow\rangle$ | $\lvert 0\rangle\lvert\downarrow\rangle$ | $\lvert -1\rangle\lvert\downarrow\rangle$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\lvert\tfrac32,\tfrac32\rangle$ | 1 | | | | | |
| $\lvert\tfrac32,\tfrac12\rangle$ | | $\sqrt{2/3}$ | $\sqrt{1/3}$ | | | |
| $\lvert\tfrac12,\tfrac12\rangle$ | | $-\sqrt{1/3}$ | $\sqrt{2/3}$ | | | |
| $\lvert\tfrac32,-\tfrac12\rangle$ | | | | $\sqrt{1/3}$ | $\sqrt{2/3}$ | |
| $\lvert\tfrac12,-\tfrac12\rangle$ | | | | $-\sqrt{2/3}$ | $\sqrt{1/3}$ | |
| $\lvert\tfrac32,-\tfrac32\rangle$ | | | | | | 1 |

The spin-orbit operator is diagonal in the coupled basis:

$$\hat{\mathbf L}\cdot\hat{\mathbf S} = \tfrac12\left(\hat J^2 - \hat L^2 - \hat S^2\right) = \tfrac{\hbar^2}{2}\left[j(j+1) - l(l+1) - \tfrac34\right] = \begin{cases}+\tfrac12 l\hbar^2 & j = l + \tfrac12\\ -\tfrac12(l+1)\hbar^2 & j = l - \tfrac12,\end{cases}$$

so a spin-orbit energy $\xi\,\hat{\mathbf L}\cdot\hat{\mathbf S}$ splits the two levels by $\tfrac12(2l + 1)\xi\hbar^2$. Hence fine-structure levels are labelled by $j$, not by $m_l$ and $m_s$.

The following script builds $\hat J^2$ for $1\otimes\tfrac12$ in the product basis with Kronecker products and diagonalises it ($\hbar = 1$); the eigenvalues $\tfrac34$ (twice) and $\tfrac{15}4$ (four times) confirm $j = \tfrac12$ and $\tfrac32$, and the eigenvectors reproduce the table up to sign.

```python
import numpy as np
def jmats(j):
    m = np.arange(j, -j - 1, -1)                      # m = j, j-1, ..., -j
    jp = np.diag(np.sqrt(j*(j + 1) - m[1:]*(m[1:] + 1)), 1)
    return [(jp + jp.T)/2, (jp - jp.T)/(2*1j), np.diag(m)]
L, S = jmats(1), jmats(0.5)
J = [np.kron(a, np.eye(2)) + np.kron(np.eye(3), b) for a, b in zip(L, S)]
J2 = sum(Ji @ Ji for Ji in J)
print(np.round(np.linalg.eigvalsh(J2), 6))           # [0.75 0.75 3.75 3.75 3.75 3.75]
```

## Key Ideas

- **Two complete bases**: uncoupled $|m_1 m_2\rangle$ diagonalises the individual projections; coupled $|j m\rangle$ diagonalises $\hat J^2$ and $\hat J_z$.
- **Triangle rule**: $|j_1 - j_2| \leq j \leq j_1 + j_2$ in integer steps, with $m = m_1 + m_2$.
- **Clebsch–Gordan coefficients** are the unitary change-of-basis matrix, generated by lowering from the top state and imposing orthogonality.
- **Triplet and singlet**: two spin-1/2 particles give a symmetric $S = 1$ triplet and an antisymmetric $S = 0$ singlet.
- **Scalar products** $\hat{\mathbf S}_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf S}_2$ and $\hat{\mathbf L}\cdot\hat{\mathbf S}$ are diagonal in the coupled basis, which is why interactions select it.
- **Fine structure** splits $j = l \pm \tfrac12$ by $\tfrac12(2l + 1)\xi\hbar^2$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Hyperfine dynamics of the hydrogen ground state

The electron and proton spins in hydrogen interact through $\hat H_{\rm hf} = A\,\hat{\mathbf S}_e\cdot\hat{\mathbf S}_p/\hbar^2$, with the triplet–singlet splitting $A = 5.874\ \mu$eV. The atom is prepared in $|{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle$ (electron up, proton down). Decompose this state, find the probability of each total spin, and the period with which the state oscillates between $|{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle$ and $|{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle$.

**Solution.** Adding and subtracting the expressions for $|1,0\rangle$ and $|0,0\rangle$ gives $|{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle = (|1,0\rangle + |0,0\rangle)/\sqrt2$, so $P(S = 1) = P(S = 0) = \tfrac12$. The two components evolve with energies differing by $A$, so the relative phase advances at $\omega = A/\hbar$ and the state returns after

$$T = \frac{h}{A} = \frac{4.136\times10^{-15}\ \text{eV s}}{5.874\times10^{-6}\ \text{eV}} = 7.04\times10^{-10}\ \text{s}.$$

The corresponding frequency $1/T = 1420$ MHz and wavelength $c/f = 21.1$ cm are those of the hydrogen line used to map galactic hydrogen.

### Example 2 — Spin content of a $p_{3/2}$ state

An electron is in $|l = 1; j = \tfrac32, m = \tfrac12\rangle$. Find the probabilities of $S_z = \pm\hbar/2$ and the expectation values $\langle S_z\rangle$ and $\langle L_z\rangle$.

**Solution.** From the table, $|\tfrac32,\tfrac12\rangle = \sqrt{2/3}\,|0\rangle|{\uparrow}\rangle + \sqrt{1/3}\,|1\rangle|{\downarrow}\rangle$. So $P(S_z = +\hbar/2) = \tfrac23$ and $P(S_z = -\hbar/2) = \tfrac13$. Then

$$\langle S_z\rangle = \tfrac\hbar2\left(\tfrac23 - \tfrac13\right) = \tfrac\hbar6, \qquad \langle L_z\rangle = 0\cdot\tfrac23 + \hbar\cdot\tfrac13 = \tfrac\hbar3.$$

Check: $\langle L_z\rangle + \langle S_z\rangle = \hbar/2 = m\hbar$. In general $\langle S_z\rangle = m\hbar/(2l + 1)$ for $j = l + \tfrac12$, a result used for the Landé $g$-factor in Lesson m3-l1.

### Example 3 — Spin-orbit constant of sodium from the D lines

The sodium D lines from $3p_{3/2}$ and $3p_{1/2}$ to $3s_{1/2}$ lie at 588.995 nm and 589.592 nm. Find the fine-structure splitting and the spin-orbit constant $\xi\hbar^2$.

**Solution.** $\Delta E = hc\,\Delta\lambda/\lambda^2 = 1239.84\ \text{eV nm}\times0.597\ \text{nm}/(589.29\ \text{nm})^2 = 2.13\times10^{-3}$ eV, or 17.2 cm$^{-1}$. For $l = 1$, $\langle\hat{\mathbf L}\cdot\hat{\mathbf S}\rangle = +\tfrac12\hbar^2$ for $j = \tfrac32$ and $-\hbar^2$ for $j = \tfrac12$, a difference of $\tfrac32\hbar^2$. Hence

$$\xi\hbar^2 = \frac{2.13\ \text{meV}}{3/2} = 1.42\ \text{meV}.$$

The $j = \tfrac32$ level lies $\tfrac12\xi\hbar^2 = 0.71$ meV above the unperturbed $3p$ energy and $j = \tfrac12$ lies 1.42 meV below, so the centre of gravity weighted by $2j + 1$ is unshifted: $4(0.71) + 2(-1.42) = 0$.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"$|{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle$ has total spin zero because the spins are opposite."** It is an equal superposition of $S = 1$ and $S = 0$; only the antisymmetric combination is the singlet.
- **"In the coupled basis $m_l$ and $m_s$ are still definite."** Only $m = m_l + m_s$ is; Example 2 shows a state with definite $j$ and $m$ but uncertain $m_l$ and $m_s$.
- **"Clebsch–Gordan signs are physical."** Overall phases are a convention (Condon–Shortley); relative signs within one state matter, but changing a multiplet's overall sign changes nothing measurable.
- **"Coupling two angular momenta always gives $j_1 + j_2$."** All values from $|j_1 - j_2|$ to $j_1 + j_2$ occur, and the state count must match the product dimension.
- **"The coupled basis is always the right basis."** It is right when $\hat{\mathbf J}_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf J}_2$-type interactions dominate; in a strong external field the uncoupled basis is better (the Paschen–Back regime).

## Connections

- The change of basis is the unitary transformation of linear-algebra Lesson m2-l1, and the Kronecker product construction in the script is the tensor product of linear-algebra.
- Term symbols $^{2S+1}L_J$, fine structure and the Zeeman pattern of atomic-and-molecular-physics Lesson m1-l2 are direct applications of the coupled basis.
- The 21 cm hyperfine line maps neutral hydrogen in the Milky Way and in distant galaxies, giving galactic rotation curves used in astrophysics-iv Lesson m1-l1.
- Spin-orbit coupling of nucleons, with the opposite sign to atoms and much larger magnitude, produces the magic numbers of nuclear-physics Lesson m1-l3.
- Singlet and triplet states of two spins are the entangled Bell states of quantum information, and their exchange symmetry drives the magnetism discussed in solid-state-physics.

## Takeaway

- Total angular momentum is an angular momentum; its eigenstates are superpositions of product states with $m_1 + m_2 = m$.
- The triangle rule fixes the allowed $j$, and lowering plus orthogonality generates all Clebsch–Gordan coefficients.
- Two spin-1/2 particles form a symmetric triplet and an antisymmetric singlet split by any $\hat{\mathbf S}_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf S}_2$ interaction.
- The $l\otimes\tfrac12$ coefficients explain fine structure and underlie the Landé $g$-factor.
- Exchange symmetry of these coupled states becomes decisive for identical particles in Lesson m2-l3.
