# Applications — Medicine, Energy, Dating

## Overview

Nuclear physics has produced some of the most consequential technologies of the modern world. In medicine, radioisotopes are used for imaging (PET, SPECT) and therapy (brachytherapy, external beam). In energy, nuclear fission provides about 10% of the world's electricity from a relatively small environmental footprint, and nuclear fusion is the long-term hope for a nearly limitless clean energy source. In dating, radioactive decay is the basis of geological and archaeological chronology, giving the age of the earth, the age of the universe, and the timeline of human evolution. This lesson closes the nuclear-physics sequence by surveying these applications, illustrating the practical impact of the physics developed in earlier lessons and pointing to the role that nuclear techniques will play in the future.

## Learning Path

- **What you should already know**: the radioactive decay law and decay chains (Lesson m2-l2); the energetics of fission and fusion (Lesson m2-l3); the operation of detectors, accelerators, and reactors (Lesson m3-l2).
- **What this lesson adds**: the use of radioisotopes in medical imaging and therapy; the operation of nuclear fission and fusion power plants; the application of radioactive dating in geology, archaeology, and cosmology.
- **What this lesson unlocks**: the broader view of nuclear physics as a foundational technology; the connections to public policy, environmental science, and the future energy mix.

## Core Explanation

### Nuclear medicine

Nuclear medicine uses radioisotopes for imaging, diagnosis, and therapy. The two main imaging modalities are PET and SPECT.

**Positron emission tomography (PET)** uses positron-emitting isotopes with short half-lives: $^{18}$F ($T_{1/2} = 110$ min), $^{11}$C (20 min), $^{13}$N (10 min), $^{15}$O (2 min). The most common is $^{18}$F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG), a glucose analogue that is taken up by metabolically active tissues (especially tumours and the brain). The positron emitted by $^{18}$F annihilates with an electron in the body, producing two 511 keV photons emitted back-to-back. Detectors in a ring around the patient record the coincident photons, and the annihilation point is reconstructed by tomographic methods. The resulting image shows the metabolic activity of the tissue with millimetre resolution.

**Single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT)** uses longer-lived gamma-emitting isotopes: $^{99m}$Tc ($T_{1/2} = 6$ hours), $^{123}$I (13 hours), $^{111}$In (2.8 days). The most common is $^{99m}$Tc, which is eluted from a $^{99}$Mo/$^{99m}$Tc generator and attached to a pharmaceutical that targets the tissue of interest. The emitted gamma ray (140 keV for $^{99m}$Tc) is detected by a gamma camera that rotates around the patient, and the image is reconstructed tomographically.

**Radiotherapy** uses ionising radiation to kill cancer cells. The most common forms are:

- **External beam radiotherapy**: high-energy photons (from linear accelerators) or charged particles (electrons, protons, or carbon ions) directed at the tumour. The dose is fractionated over many sessions to spare healthy tissue.

- **Brachytherapy**: sealed radioactive sources (typically $^{192}$Ir, $^{125}$I, or $^{103}$Pd) implanted directly in or near the tumour. The advantage is a high local dose with rapid falloff outside the target.

- **Radioisotope therapy**: unsealed radioactive sources that target specific tissues. Examples: $^{131}$I for thyroid cancer (the thyroid concentrates iodine), $^{177}$Lu-DOTATATE for neuroendocrine tumours, $^{223}$Ra for bone metastases.

- **Proton therapy**: protons with energies of 70–250 MeV have a sharp Bragg peak at a controllable depth, allowing the dose to be concentrated in the tumour while sparing tissue behind it. Carbon-ion therapy is similar but with even greater biological effectiveness.

The dose absorbed by tissue is measured in grays (Gy, J/kg) or sieverts (Sv, with a quality factor for the radiation type). A typical radiotherapy prescription is 60–80 Gy delivered in 2 Gy fractions over 6–7 weeks.

### Nuclear energy

**Nuclear fission power** provides about 10% of the world's electricity (about 20% in OECD countries, 70% in France). A typical 1 GWe fission power plant uses about 200 tonnes of enriched uranium per year and produces about 20 tonnes of spent fuel. The energy density of fission is about $8 \times 10^{13}$ J/kg, eight orders of magnitude larger than chemical energy.

The advantages of fission power:

- **Low carbon emissions**: comparable to wind and solar in lifecycle terms.
- **High energy density**: a small amount of fuel produces a large amount of energy.
- **Dispatchable**: provides baseload power that can be adjusted on demand.
- **Mature technology**: 70+ years of operational experience.

The challenges:

- **Radioactive waste**: spent fuel remains radioactive for tens of thousands of years. Geological disposal in deep repositories is the favoured long-term solution.
- **Proliferation risk**: the same technology that produces power can produce material for nuclear weapons.
- **Capital cost**: nuclear power plants are expensive to build, though their operating costs are low.
- **Accident risk**: the accidents at Three Mile Island (1979), Chernobyl (1986), and Fukushima (2011) demonstrated the catastrophic potential of severe accidents.
- **Public acceptance**: opposition to nuclear power is strong in many countries, slowing new construction.

**Nuclear fusion power** has been a research goal since the 1950s. The two main approaches are:

- **Magnetic confinement fusion**: tokamaks and stellarators confine a hot plasma (about 10$^8$ K) using strong magnetic fields. The leading devices are ITER (under construction in France), JET (UK, recently decommissioned), and the National Ignition Facility (NIF, USA, which uses inertial confinement). In December 2022, NIF achieved scientific break-even, releasing more energy from fusion than was delivered to the fuel; this was a major milestone.

- **Inertial confinement fusion**: a small capsule of deuterium–tritium fuel is compressed and heated by intense laser or particle beams. The fusion reactions occur before the fuel can fly apart. NIF uses 192 laser beams to deliver about 2 MJ of energy to a pepper-corn-sized capsule, igniting a fusion burn that releases about 3 MJ.

The advantages of fusion power:

- **Virtually limitless fuel**: deuterium is extracted from seawater; tritium is bred from lithium in the reactor blanket.
- **No long-lived radioactive waste**: the activated structural materials have half-lives of decades, not millennia.
- **No meltdown risk**: the plasma is so dilute that a loss of confinement simply extinguishes the reaction.
- **No proliferation risk**: the fuel cycle does not produce fissile material suitable for weapons.

The challenges:

- **Plasma confinement**: holding a 10$^8$ K plasma stable long enough for fusion to release net energy is the central physics problem.
- **Materials**: the neutron flux from D–T fusion activates and damages the structural materials of the reactor.
- **Tritium breeding**: the reactor must breed its own tritium from lithium in a surrounding blanket.
- **Capital cost**: a commercial fusion power plant is expected to be more expensive than a fission plant, though the operating costs would be lower.
- **Timeline**: commercial fusion power is probably 20–50 years away, despite the recent progress.

### Radioactive dating

Radioactive dating is the use of radioactive decay to determine the age of a sample. The most important systems are:

**Radiocarbon (¹⁴C) dating** is used for organic materials up to about 50,000 years old. $^{14}$C is produced in the upper atmosphere by cosmic-ray spallation of $^{14}$N:

$$n + ^{14}\text{N} \to ^{14}\text{C} + p.$$

The $^{14}$C oxidises to CO$_2$ and is incorporated into living organisms. After the organism dies, the $^{14}$C decays with no replenishment:

$$^{14}\text{C} \to ^{14}\text{N} + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e, \quad T_{1/2} = 5730\,\text{years}.$$

The ratio of $^{14}$C to $^{12}$C in the sample is measured by accelerator mass spectrometry (AMS), and the age is computed from the decay law. Calibration with tree rings and other archives is required to correct for past variations in atmospheric $^{14}$C.

Radiocarbon dating is the workhorse of archaeology; it has been used to date the Shroud of Turin, the Dead Sea Scrolls, and the cave paintings of Chauvet and Lascaux. The technique was developed by Willard Libby in the late 1940s, earning him the 1960 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

**K–Ar and Ar–Ar dating** are used for volcanic rocks from about 100,000 years to billions of years. $^{40}$K ($T_{1/2} = 1.25 \times 10^9$ years) decays to $^{40}$Ar (a stable noble gas) and $^{40}$Ca. The K–Ar clock is set when the rock solidifies, trapping the argon. The Ar–Ar method improves on K–Ar by neutron irradiation to convert $^{39}$K to $^{39}$Ar, which serves as a proxy for the original potassium content.

**U–Pb dating** is the gold standard for very old rocks and the age of the earth. $^{238}$U ($T_{1/2} = 4.47 \times 10^9$ years) decays to $^{206}$Pb, and $^{235}$U ($T_{1/2} = 0.704 \times 10^9$ years) decays to $^{207}$Pb. The two clocks provide a self-consistency check; concordia diagrams plot the two Pb isotope ratios against each other and identify undisturbed samples. The oldest known minerals, zircons from Jack Hills in Western Australia, have been dated to about 4.4 billion years, providing a lower limit on the age of the earth. The age of the earth itself is 4.54 billion years, established by U–Pb dating of meteorites that have not been geologically processed.

**Other dating systems**: Rb–Sr ($^{87}$Rb to $^{87}$Sr, $T_{1/2} = 49 \times 10^9$ years), Sm–Nd ($^{147}$Sm to $^{143}$Nd, $T_{1/2} = 1.06 \times 10^{11}$ years), Re–Os ($^{187}$Re to $^{187}$Os, $T_{1/2} = 4.3 \times 10^{10}$ years), and Lu–Hf ($^{176}$Lu to $^{176}$Hf, $T_{1/2} = 3.7 \times 10^{10}$ years). Each system has its own range of applicability and its own geological context.

**Cosmogenic isotopes**: $^{10}$Be ($T_{1/2} = 1.4 \times 10^6$ years), $^{26}$Al ($7.2 \times 10^5$ years), $^{36}$Cl ($3.0 \times 10^5$ years), $^{21}$Ne (stable) are produced by cosmic-ray spallation in surface rocks. The concentration of these isotopes in a rock sample gives the surface-exposure age, the time since the rock was exposed to cosmic rays. This technique is used to date glacial moraines, river terraces, lava flows, and meteorite falls.

### Stellar nucleosynthesis

The same nuclear reactions that power nuclear reactors and the sun have produced the chemical elements in the universe. Stellar nucleosynthesis proceeds by several processes:

- **Big Bang nucleosynthesis**: produced H, He, and a trace of Li in the first three minutes after the Big Bang. The mass fractions are about 75% H, 25% He, and $10^{-9}$ Li.

- **Stellar burning**: hydrogen burning (pp-chain and CNO cycle) produces He; helium burning (triple-alpha) produces C and O; carbon burning produces Ne, Na, Mg; neon burning produces O, Mg; oxygen burning produces Si, S, Ar, Ca; silicon burning produces the iron-peak elements (Fe, Ni, Co, Cr, Mn).

- **The s-process (slow neutron capture)**: in red giants and asymptotic giant branch (AGB) stars, neutron captures are slow compared with beta decay, producing about half of the isotopes heavier than iron (up to $^{209}$Bi).

- **The r-process (rapid neutron capture)**: in supernovae and neutron-star mergers, neutron captures are fast compared with beta decay, producing the other half of the heavy isotopes, including uranium and thorium. The discovery of GW170817, the binary neutron-star merger detected by LIGO and Virgo in 2017, confirmed that neutron-star mergers are a major site of r-process nucleosynthesis.

- **The p-process (proton capture)**: produces the proton-rich isotopes that cannot be made by the s- or r-processes. The site is uncertain; core-collapse supernovae and thermonuclear supernovae are candidates.

The combination of stellar nucleosynthesis and radioactive dating gives a self-consistent picture: the age of the universe (13.8 billion years, from cosmology), the age of the galaxy (about the same), the age of the solar system (4.567 billion years, from U–Pb dating of meteorites), and the age of the earth (4.54 billion years, from U–Pb dating of terrestrial and meteoritic samples).

## Key Ideas

- **Nuclear medicine**: PET uses positron emitters ($^{18}$F, $^{11}$C, $^{15}$O); SPECT uses gamma emitters ($^{99m}$Tc, $^{123}$I); radiotherapy uses external beams, brachytherapy, or radioisotopes.
- **Nuclear fission power**: 10% of world electricity, low carbon, dispatchable, with challenges of waste, proliferation, and accidents.
- **Nuclear fusion power**: ITER (magnetic confinement) and NIF (inertial confinement) are the leading approaches; net energy gain was achieved at NIF in 2022.
- **Radiocarbon dating**: $^{14}$C is produced in the atmosphere, incorporated into living organisms, and decays after death; range up to 50,000 years.
- **U–Pb dating**: the gold standard for the oldest rocks and the age of the earth (4.54 billion years).
- **Cosmogenic isotopes**: produced by cosmic rays in surface rocks; used for surface-exposure dating.
- **Stellar nucleosynthesis**: the s-process in AGB stars, the r-process in supernovae and neutron-star mergers, the p-process in supernovae.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Dose from a PET scan

A 70 kg patient receives a 10 mCi injection of $^{18}$F-FDG. The effective dose equivalent is about 0.025 mSv/MBq. Compute the effective dose in mSv.

**Solution.** 10 mCi = 10 × 3.7 × 10$^7$ Bq = 3.7 × 10$^8$ Bq = 370 MBq. The effective dose is

$$H = 0.025 \times 370 = 9.25\,\text{mSv}.$$

This is comparable to a few years of natural background radiation, and far below the threshold for deterministic effects.

### Example 2 — Energy from 1 kg of uranium

A typical fission releases 200 MeV per $^{235}$U nucleus. Compute the energy from 1 kg of $^{235}$U, and compare to the energy from 1 kg of coal (about 24 MJ/kg).

**Solution.** The number of atoms in 1 kg of $^{235}$U is

$$N = \frac{1000}{235} \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = 2.56 \times 10^{24}.$$

The total energy is

$$E = 200 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-13} \times 2.56 \times 10^{24} = 8.2 \times 10^{13}\,\text{J} = 82\,\text{TJ}.$$

This is equivalent to about 3.4 million tonnes of coal, or about 24 GWh of electricity (assuming 33% thermal efficiency). The energy density of fission is about $3.4 \times 10^6$ times that of coal.

### Example 3 — Radiocarbon age of a sample

A wooden artefact has a $^{14}$C/$^{12}$C ratio of 0.6 of the modern value. Estimate the age.

**Solution.** Using the decay law with $T_{1/2} = 5730$ years:

$$t = T_{1/2} \frac{\ln(N_0/N)}{\ln 2} = 5730 \times \frac{\ln(1/0.6)}{\ln 2} = 5730 \times \frac{0.511}{0.693} \approx 4220\,\text{years}.$$

This is roughly the age of the Egyptian pyramids or the Early Bronze Age in the Levant, depending on the calibration curve used.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"PET scans are dangerous."** The radiation dose from a PET scan is about 10 mSv, comparable to a few years of natural background radiation. The benefits of accurate diagnosis far outweigh the small risk.
- **"Nuclear power is unsafe."** Modern reactor designs have multiple redundant safety systems. The death rate per unit electricity from nuclear power is among the lowest of any energy source, comparable to wind and solar.
- **"Nuclear waste has no solution."** Geological disposal in deep repositories is the favoured long-term solution, and several countries (Finland, Sweden, France) are well advanced in site selection and construction. The waste is a manageable problem, not an existential one.
- **"Fusion is 30 years away, as it always has been."** This is a tired joke. Significant progress has been made: the confinement time in tokamaks has improved by orders of magnitude, and NIF achieved scientific break-even in 2022. The timeline is shortening, though commercial fusion is still probably decades away.
- **"Radiocarbon dating gives the exact age."** The raw radiocarbon age must be calibrated with tree rings and other archives to give a calendar age. The accuracy depends on the quality of the calibration curve, which is now very good back to about 13,900 years.

## Connections

- Nuclear medicine, fission power, and radioactive dating are the three most consequential applications of nuclear physics, each with its own scientific basis and societal impact.
- The same nuclear reactions (D–T, D–D, D–$^{3}$He) are studied in the laboratory, in stars, and in fusion reactors; the cross-sections are the same.
- The age of the earth, the age of the universe, and the timeline of human evolution are all established by nuclear-physics techniques; the consistency of these ages is one of the great achievements of modern science.
- Stellar nucleosynthesis connects nuclear physics to astrophysics and cosmology; the abundance pattern of the elements is a fossil record of nuclear processes in stars.
- The future of nuclear energy — advanced fission reactors, fusion, and possibly accelerator-driven systems — depends on the same basic physics developed in this course.

## Takeaway

- Nuclear medicine uses radioisotopes for imaging (PET, SPECT) and therapy (external beam, brachytherapy, radioisotope therapy).
- Nuclear fission provides 10% of world electricity; nuclear fusion is the long-term hope for clean, abundant energy.
- Radioactive dating is the foundation of geological and archaeological chronology, from radiocarbon (50,000 years) to U–Pb (the age of the earth).
- Stellar nucleosynthesis explains the origin of the chemical elements; the s-, r-, and p-processes each contribute to the abundance pattern.
- Nuclear physics is a foundational science with applications that touch nearly every aspect of modern life.
