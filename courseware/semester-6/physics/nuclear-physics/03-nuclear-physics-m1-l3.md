# Shell Model and Magic Numbers

## Overview

The liquid drop model and the semi-empirical mass formula capture the smooth, average behaviour of nuclear binding energies, but they miss important systematic deviations. Certain numbers of protons or neutrons — 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126 — produce nuclei that are extra stable, extra abundant, and have systematically lower masses than the SEMF predicts. These are the **magic numbers**, and they reveal the existence of a shell structure in nuclei analogous to the electron shells in atoms. The nuclear shell model, developed in the late 1940s by Maria Goeppert Mayer and J. Hans D. Jensen, treats each nucleon as moving in a mean-field potential generated by all the other nucleons, with a strong spin–orbit coupling that splits the levels and produces the observed magic numbers. The shell model explains nuclear spins, parities, magnetic moments, beta-decay rates, and the existence of "islands" of unusual structure far from stability. The lesson closes the nuclear-properties sequence by giving a working picture of the quantum mechanics of nuclei.

## Learning Path

- **What you should already know**: the Schrödinger equation in a central potential and the hydrogen-like atomic orbitals (introductory quantum mechanics, Semester 4); the spin–orbit interaction in atomic physics (atomic and molecular physics, Module 1); the SEMF and the gross features of nuclear binding (Lesson m1-l2).

## Core Explanation

### Evidence for magic numbers

Several independent lines of evidence point to the special stability of nuclei with certain proton and neutron numbers:

- **Abundance**: nuclei with $Z = 8$ ($^{16}$O), $Z = 20$ ($^{40}$Ca), $Z = 50$ ($^{120}$Sn, the most abundant stable tin isotope), $Z = 82$ ($^{208}$Pb) are extraordinarily abundant in the solar system. The same is true for $N = 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126$.

- **Binding energy**: the binding energy of magic nuclei is higher than the SEMF predicts, with a discontinuity at the magic number. The "shell correction" to the SEMF is about 1–2 MeV.

- **Separation energies**: the energy required to remove a single nucleon from a magic nucleus is anomalously large. The one-neutron separation energy $S_n$ drops sharply after passing through a magic neutron number.

- **Beta-decay lifetimes**: nuclei just above a magic number have anomalously long beta-decay half-lives, because the decay would have to populate a level in the daughter nucleus with a different shell structure.

- **First excited states**: magic nuclei have anomalously high first excited states. $^{208}$Pb, with $Z = 82$ and $N = 126$ (doubly magic), has its first excited state at 2.6 MeV, more than twice the typical value of 1 MeV for heavy nuclei.

The magic numbers are:

$$2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126.$$

For protons, the magic numbers are 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82 (and possibly 114 for superheavy elements). For neutrons, the same up to 82, plus 126. The nucleus $^{208}$Pb ($Z = 82$, $N = 126$) is doubly magic and particularly stable; it is the end of several decay chains and the heaviest stable (or nearly stable) nucleus.

### Independent-particle picture

The nuclear shell model takes seriously the idea that each nucleon moves in a mean-field potential generated by all the other nucleons. This is the same conceptual step that takes us from the hydrogen atom to many-electron atoms: in atomic physics, the Hartree–Fock mean field gives the electron shell structure, and the residual interaction between electrons is treated as a perturbation.

The nucleon–nucleon strong force is short-ranged and attractive, with a hard core at very short distances. The mean-field potential is therefore roughly constant in the interior of the nucleus (because the short-range force acts only between nearest neighbours) and falls off in a surface region of width about 1 fm. A common approximation is the **three-dimensional isotropic harmonic oscillator** for the interior plus an infinite wall at the surface, often called the "wine-bottle" potential. The energy levels of a 3D isotropic harmonic oscillator are

$$E_N = (N + 3/2)\hbar\omega, \quad N = 2(n - 1) + \ell,$$

where $N$ is the principal quantum number, $n$ is the radial quantum number, and $\ell$ is the orbital angular momentum. The degeneracies are

$$g_N = \frac{(N+1)(N+2)}{2},$$

giving the cumulative occupation numbers $2, 8, 20, 40, 70, 112, \ldots$ (each level holds $2(2\ell + 1)$ nucleons because of the two spin states). The first three magic numbers — 2, 8, 20 — are reproduced, but the higher ones are not.

### Spin–orbit coupling

The breakthrough of Mayer and Jensen (1949) was to recognise that a strong spin–orbit coupling in nuclei splits each $\ell$ level into $j = \ell + 1/2$ (lower) and $j = \ell - 1/2$ (higher), with a splitting that grows with $\ell$. The strength of the spin–orbit coupling is much larger in nuclei than in atoms because the nuclear mean field is steeper than the atomic Coulomb potential.

The spin–orbit Hamiltonian is

$$H_{SO} = -V_{SO} \mathbf{L} \cdot \mathbf{S},$$

with $V_{SO}$ positive (so that the $j = \ell + 1/2$ level lies below the $j = \ell - 1/2$ level by an amount that increases with $\ell$).

The combined effect of the harmonic-oscillator levels and the spin–orbit splitting gives the observed magic numbers. The standard ordering, with cumulative occupation numbers, is:

- $1s_{1/2}$: 2.
- $1p_{3/2}, 1p_{1/2}$: 2 + 4 = 6, total 8.
- $1d_{5/2}, 1d_{3/2}, 2s_{1/2}$: 6 + 4 + 2 = 12, total 20.
- $1f_{7/2}$: 8, total 28.
- $2p_{3/2}, 1f_{5/2}, 2p_{1/2}, 1g_{9/2}$: 4 + 6 + 2 + 10 = 22, total 50.
- $1g_{7/2}, 2d_{5/2}, 2d_{3/2}, 3s_{1/2}, 1h_{11/2}$: 8 + 6 + 4 + 2 + 12 = 32, total 82.
- $1h_{9/2}, 2f_{7/2}, 2f_{5/2}, 3p_{3/2}, 3p_{1/2}, 1i_{13/2}$: 10 + 8 + 6 + 4 + 2 + 14 = 44, total 126.

The notation $n\ell_j$ gives the radial quantum number, the orbital angular momentum, and the total angular momentum. The label $s, p, d, f, g, h, i, j$ corresponds to $\ell = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7$.

The spin–orbit splitting of the $1g$ level into $1g_{9/2}$ (10 nucleons) and $1g_{7/2}$ (8 nucleons) is what pushes the cumulative total from 50 to 82 and 126. Without spin–orbit coupling, the magic numbers would stop at 20.

### Predictions of the shell model

The shell model makes several predictions that are tested in essentially every nuclear spectroscopy experiment:

- **Ground-state spin and parity**: a nucleus with all closed shells plus a single nucleon has the spin and parity of that single nucleon. So $^{17}$O (closed $^{16}$O plus one neutron) has $J^\pi = 5/2^+$, the spin-parity of the $1d_{5/2}$ neutron orbital.

- **Magnetic moment**: a single nucleon outside closed shells has a magnetic moment given by the Schmidt limits, with two branches ($j = \ell + 1/2$ and $j = \ell - 1/2$) that are quite different. Measured moments fall between the limits because of configuration mixing.

- **Electric quadrupole moment**: a single nucleon outside closed shells has a small quadrupole moment; a nucleus with many nucleons outside closed shells (in the middle of a shell) has a large quadrupole moment that signals collective deformation.

- **Islands of inversion**: certain regions of the nuclear chart show ground-state configurations that differ from the shell-model prediction, indicating that the naive shell structure is broken. The classic example is the "island of inversion" around $^{32}$Mg, where the ground state is dominated by intruder configurations (two neutrons promoted across the $N = 20$ shell gap). The existence of such islands shows that the shell structure is robust but not inviolable.

- **Spectroscopic factors**: in transfer reactions (such as $(d, p)$), the cross-section for populating a particular final state is proportional to the square of the overlap with a shell-model wavefunction. Spectroscopic factors are the standard way to identify single-particle states.

### Beyond the simple shell model

The simple shell model is a starting point. Several refinements are needed for quantitative work:

- **Residual interactions**: the mean field is only an approximation; the residual interaction between valence nucleons splits the single-particle levels into a multiplet of states.
- **Configuration interaction**: the true ground state is a superposition of many shell-model configurations; the calculation of this superposition is a major computational problem.
- **Collective models**: for nuclei with many valence nucleons (in the middle of a shell), the simple shell model becomes intractable, and collective models (the rotational and vibrational models of Bohr and Mottelson) are more appropriate.
- **Relativistic effects**: for heavy nuclei, the spin–orbit coupling and the scalar and vector fields of the relativistic mean field are important.

The unified description of nuclear structure uses a combination of these approaches, with the shell model serving as the foundation.

## Key Ideas

- **Magic numbers** are 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126. Nuclei with these proton or neutron numbers are extra stable.
- **Nuclear shell model** treats each nucleon as moving in a mean field; the structure of the levels is determined by the central potential and the spin–orbit coupling.
- **Spin–orbit coupling** is strong in nuclei; it splits each $\ell$ level into $j = \ell \pm 1/2$, with the $j = \ell + 1/2$ level lower.
- **Closed shells** give spherical nuclei with high first excited states and small quadrupole moments; **mid-shell** nuclei are deformed with large quadrupole moments.
- **Shell-model predictions** of spin, parity, magnetic moment, and spectroscopic factors are tested in essentially every nuclear spectroscopy experiment.
- **Islands of inversion** are regions of the nuclear chart where the simple shell structure is broken.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Ground-state spin of $^{17}$O

Predict the ground-state spin and parity of $^{17}$O using the shell model.

**Solution.** $^{16}$O is doubly magic with $Z = 8$ and $N = 8$. $^{17}$O has one extra neutron, which goes into the next available level. After the $N = 8$ shell (closed at $1p_{1/2}$), the next level is $1d_{5/2}$. The single neutron occupies this level, so the ground state has $J^\pi = 5/2^+$. The experimental value is indeed $5/2^+$.

### Example 2 — Magnetic moment of $^{17}$O

Predict the magnetic moment of $^{17}$O using the Schmidt limits for a $d_{5/2}$ neutron.

**Solution.** The Schmidt magnetic moment for a single nucleon with $j = \ell + 1/2$ is

$$\mu = \left[g_l \ell + \frac{g_s}{2}\right] \mu_N,$$

where $g_l$ is the orbital $g$-factor and $g_s$ is the spin $g$-factor. For a neutron, $g_l = 0$ and $g_s = 2 \mu_n = -3.826$ (i.e. $\mu_n = -1.913$). For $d_{5/2}$ ($j = 5/2, \ell = 2, j = \ell + 1/2$):

$$\mu = \left[0 \times 2 + \frac{-3.826}{2}\right] \mu_N = -1.913\,\mu_N.$$

The experimental value is $-1.893\,\mu_N$, in good agreement with the Schmidt limit.

### Example 3 — Predicting the magic nucleus $^{208}$Pb

Identify the closed shells in $^{208}$Pb and verify its doubly magic nature.

**Solution.** $^{208}$Pb has $Z = 82$ and $N = 126$. The shell-model level scheme gives:

- For protons, the cumulative occupation number at $Z = 82$ corresponds to a closed shell at the $1h_{11/2}$ level.
- For neutrons, the cumulative occupation number at $N = 126$ corresponds to a closed shell at the $1i_{13/2}$ level.

The first excited state of $^{208}$Pb is at 2.6 MeV (a $3^-$ collective octupole vibration), well above the typical 1 MeV. The neutron separation energy is about 7.4 MeV, well above the trend. The isotope is the end of several decay chains and the heaviest nearly-stable (or strictly stable) nucleus, with $T_{1/2}$ effectively infinite.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Magic numbers come from a fundamental quantum effect."** They come from the spin–orbit coupling in a nuclear mean field, which is a particular feature of the strong force. The exact values are not derivable from first principles; they are empirical inputs to the shell model.
- **"The shell model assumes non-interacting nucleons."** The mean field is generated by the nucleon–nucleon interaction; the assumption is that a single-particle picture is a good starting point, not that the nucleons are independent.
- **"Magic numbers are exact."** They are excellent approximations. The first deviations show up for very light nuclei (e.g. $^{11}$Li has intruder configurations) and near the drip lines (e.g. the "island of inversion" near $N = 20$).
- **"All nuclei with magic numbers are spherical."** Magic nuclei are spherical, but mid-shell nuclei (with many nucleons outside closed shells) are deformed. The shape of a nucleus depends on where it sits in the shell.
- **"The shell model is just for low-lying states."** No. The shell model is a framework for all nuclear states. Different model spaces and interactions are used for different excitation energies, but the conceptual basis is the same.

## Connections

- The shell model is the nuclear analogue of the atomic shell model; the spin–orbit coupling is much stronger in nuclei because of the steeper mean-field potential.
- The same pairing that gives the $+\delta$ term in the SEMF appears in the shell model as a seniority coupling of like nucleons, analogous to Cooper pairing in metallic superconductors.
- The deformed mid-shell nuclei are described by the collective models of Bohr and Mottelson, with rotational and vibrational spectra that complement the shell-model description.
- The islands of inversion and the structure of exotic nuclei near the drip lines are a current frontier of nuclear physics, with major facilities (FRIB, RIKEN, GSI) pushing the boundaries.
- The shell model provides the single-particle basis used in calculations of nuclear reactions, beta decay, and electron capture, which are the topics of the next module.

## Takeaway

- Magic numbers — 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126 — reveal the shell structure of nuclei, analogous to the electron shells of atoms.
- The nuclear shell model treats each nucleon as moving in a mean field; a strong spin–orbit coupling produces the observed magic numbers.
- Closed-shell nuclei are spherical with high first excited states; mid-shell nuclei are deformed.
- The shell model successfully predicts spins, parities, magnetic moments, and spectroscopic factors across the nuclear chart.
- Beyond the simple shell model, configuration interaction and collective models extend the description to deformed and exotic nuclei.
