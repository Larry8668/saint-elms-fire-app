# Particle Physics and the Standard Model

## Overview

Particle physics is the study of the most elementary constituents of matter and the forces between them. The Standard Model, developed over the second half of the twentieth century and crowned by the discovery of the Higgs boson in 2012, gives a remarkably complete description of all known particles and three of the four fundamental forces (electromagnetic, weak, and strong; gravity is not yet incorporated). This lesson lays out the structure of the Standard Model: the six quarks and six leptons, the four gauge bosons that mediate the forces, and the Higgs boson that gives mass to the weak gauge bosons and the fermions. The lesson then discusses the conservation laws that govern particle interactions and points to the open questions that drive current research: neutrino masses, dark matter, and the unification of forces.

## Learning Path

- **What you should already know**: the basic structure of the atom (atomic and molecular physics, Module 1); the radioactive decay processes involving neutrinos (Lesson m2-l1); the four fundamental forces from introductory physics.
- **What this lesson adds**: the classification of particles into fermions and bosons, hadrons and leptons; the structure of the Standard Model; the conservation laws that govern particle interactions; the role of the Higgs boson.
- **What later lessons this will unlock**: the use of particle-physics techniques in nuclear physics and astrophysics; the open questions of dark matter and neutrino masses; the application of particle detection in medicine and industry (Lesson m3-l2).

## Core Explanation

### Particles and forces

Modern physics recognises four fundamental forces:

- **Gravity**: the attraction of mass-energy, mediated by the (hypothetical) graviton. The weakest force by far, dominant only at macroscopic scales because of its long range and the universal positive mass.
- **Electromagnetism**: the interaction of electric charges, mediated by the photon. The dominant force in atomic, molecular, and condensed-matter physics.
- **Weak force**: responsible for beta decay and other slow processes, mediated by the $W^\pm$ and $Z^0$ bosons. The only force that changes quark flavour.
- **Strong force**: binds quarks into hadrons and holds the nucleus together, mediated by gluons. The strongest force at short range, but short-ranged (about 1 fm) because of colour confinement.

Each force is described by a gauge theory with a characteristic coupling constant. The Standard Model is the unified gauge theory of the strong, weak, and electromagnetic interactions, with the gravitational force left out (a fully satisfactory quantum theory of gravity is not yet known).

### Fermions and bosons

All particles are either fermions (half-integer spin) or bosons (integer spin). Fermions obey the Pauli exclusion principle; bosons do not. The elementary fermions of the Standard Model come in two classes: quarks (which feel the strong force) and leptons (which do not). The elementary bosons are the gauge bosons (photon, $W^\pm$, $Z^0$, gluons) and the Higgs boson.

**Quarks** come in six flavours: up ($u$), down ($d$), charm ($c$), strange ($s$), top ($t$), and bottom ($b$). Each quark carries a colour charge (red, green, or blue) and a fractional electric charge ($+2/3$ for $u, c, t$; $-1/3$ for $d, s, b$). Quarks are confined inside hadrons; they have never been observed as free particles.

**Leptons** come in six flavours: electron ($e^-$), muon ($\mu^-$), tau ($\tau^-$), and the three corresponding neutrinos ($\nu_e, \nu_\mu, \nu_\tau$). The charged leptons have electric charge $-1$; the neutrinos are neutral. Leptons do not feel the strong force.

The fermions are organised in three **generations**, each with two quarks and two leptons:

- 1st generation: $u, d, \nu_e, e^-$. The quarks and leptons of ordinary matter.
- 2nd generation: $c, s, \nu_\mu, \mu^-$. Found in cosmic rays and in accelerator experiments.
- 3rd generation: $t, b, \nu_\tau, \tau^-$. The heaviest, discovered in the 1970s ($t$ in 1995).

Each generation is a heavier copy of the previous one, with the same quantum numbers but different masses. The CKM matrix (for quarks) and the PMNS matrix (for neutrinos) describe the mixing between the generations.

### Hadrons

Quarks combine into colour-neutral bound states called hadrons. The two main classes are:

- **Baryons**: three quarks (or three antiquarks). The proton is $uud$, the neutron is $udd$, the $\Delta^{++}$ is $uuu$. Baryons have half-integer spin and obey the Pauli principle; they are fermions.
- **Mesons**: one quark and one antiquark. The pion is $u\bar{d}$ or $u\bar{u}$ or $d\bar{d}$, the kaon is $u\bar{s}$ or $s\bar{u}$, the $J/\psi$ is $c\bar{c}$. Mesons have integer spin and are bosons.

A rich spectroscopy of hadrons is known, with hundreds of states catalogued. The quark model predicted the existence of the $\Omega^-$ (sss) before its discovery, a famous success.

The masses of hadrons are mostly due to the binding energy of the strong force (the energy of the gluon field), not the bare masses of the quarks. The $u$ and $d$ quarks have bare masses of a few MeV, but the proton mass is 938 MeV — the binding energy is 99% of the mass.

### Forces as gauge theories

Each fundamental force is described by a gauge theory:

- **Quantum electrodynamics (QED)**: the gauge theory of electromagnetism, with gauge group $U(1)$. The gauge boson is the photon. The coupling constant is the fine-structure constant $\alpha \approx 1/137$.

- **Quantum chromodynamics (QCD)**: the gauge theory of the strong force, with gauge group $SU(3)$. The gauge bosons are eight gluons, each carrying a colour–anticolour combination. The coupling constant is $\alpha_s$, which depends on the momentum transfer: at high energies (short distances) it is small (asymptotic freedom), at low energies (long distances) it is large (confinement). Confinement is the reason free quarks have never been observed.

- **Electroweak theory**: the unified gauge theory of the electromagnetic and weak interactions, with gauge group $SU(2) \times U(1)$. The four gauge bosons are the $W^+, W^-, W^3$ (from $SU(2)$) and the $B$ (from $U(1)$). After electroweak symmetry breaking, these mix to give the physical $W^\pm$, $Z^0$, and photon. The $W$ and $Z$ are massive (80 GeV and 91 GeV); the photon is massless.

The **Higgs mechanism** breaks the electroweak symmetry and gives mass to the $W$ and $Z$ bosons (and to the fermions, via Yukawa couplings). The Higgs field is a scalar field with a non-zero vacuum expectation value; particles acquire mass by interacting with this field. The Higgs boson itself is a quantum of the Higgs field, observed at the LHC in 2012 with a mass of 125 GeV.

### Conservation laws

Particle interactions are governed by conservation laws derived from symmetries. The most important are:

- **Energy and momentum**: always conserved.
- **Electric charge**: always conserved.
- **Baryon number** $B$: conserved in the Standard Model. The proton is the lightest baryon, so it cannot decay (within the Standard Model), explaining the stability of ordinary matter. Proton decay would violate $B$ conservation.
- **Lepton number** $L$: separately conserved for each generation ($L_e, L_\mu, L_\tau$) in the Standard Model. Neutrino oscillations violate these individual lepton numbers, but total $L$ is conserved.
- **Strangeness** $S$: conserved in strong and electromagnetic interactions; violated in weak interactions. The strange quark $s$ has $S = -1$.
- **Parity $P$**: conserved in strong and electromagnetic interactions; maximally violated in weak interactions. The $W$ boson couples only to left-handed particles and right-handed antiparticles.
- **Charge conjugation $C$**: also violated in weak interactions. The combined $CP$ symmetry is violated at the level of $10^{-3}$ in the kaon system, providing the necessary condition for the baryon asymmetry of the universe.
- **Colour charge**: conserved in all interactions; the strong force cannot change colour.

### Open questions

The Standard Model is a complete and remarkably accurate theory, but it leaves several questions unanswered:

- **Neutrino masses**: the Standard Model originally had massless neutrinos; the discovery of neutrino oscillations shows that they have mass. The mechanism for generating neutrino masses (Dirac vs Majorana) is unknown.
- **Dark matter**: the Standard Model has no suitable dark matter candidate. The most popular candidates are weakly interacting massive particles (WIMPs) or axions, neither of which is in the Standard Model.
- **Dark energy**: the cosmological constant that drives the accelerating expansion of the universe is not explained.
- **Matter–antimatter asymmetry**: the universe contains much more matter than antimatter, but the Standard Model produces only a small excess.
- **Quantum gravity**: the Standard Model does not include gravity. String theory and loop quantum gravity are candidates for a unified theory.

These questions drive the experimental programme of particle physics today, with experiments at the Large Hadron Collider, underground neutrino detectors, dark matter searches, and cosmic microwave background observatories.

## Key Ideas

- **Fermions and bosons**: all particles are one or the other; fermions obey the Pauli principle, bosons do not.
- **Quarks and leptons**: the elementary fermions; quarks feel the strong force, leptons do not; six flavours of each in three generations.
- **Gauge bosons**: photon (electromagnetism), $W^\pm$ and $Z^0$ (weak), gluons (strong); the graviton is hypothetical.
- **Higgs boson**: gives mass to the $W$ and $Z$ bosons and to the fermions via the Higgs mechanism.
- **Conservation laws**: baryon number, lepton number, strangeness, parity, charge conjugation.
- **Open questions**: neutrino masses, dark matter, dark energy, matter–antimatter asymmetry, quantum gravity.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Identifying a particle reaction

Consider the reaction $\pi^- + p \to \Lambda^0 + K^0$. The pion is $u\bar{d}$ or $d\bar{u}$, the proton is $uud$, the $\Lambda^0$ is $uds$, and the $K^0$ is $d\bar{s}$. Verify the conservation of charge, baryon number, and strangeness.

**Solution.** Choose the $\pi^-$ as $d\bar{u}$. Initial state quarks: $d, \bar{u}$ (from $\pi^-$) plus $u, u, d$ (from $p$) — net $u, u, d, d$ with one $\bar{u}$, i.e. net $u, d, d$ (the $\bar{u}$ annihilates one $u$). Final state: $\Lambda^0 = uds$ and $K^0 = d\bar{s}$ — quarks $u, d, s, d, \bar{s}$ — net $u, d, d$ (the $s$ and $\bar{s}$ cancel). Both sides have net quark content $u, d, d$. ✓

Charge: initial: $\pi^- + p$ has charge $(-1) + (+1) = 0$. Final: $\Lambda^0 + K^0$ has charge $0 + 0 = 0$. Charge is conserved.

Baryon number: $0 + 1 = 1 + 0 = 1$. Baryon number is conserved.

Strangeness: $0 + 0 = (-1) + (+1) = 0$. Strangeness is conserved on both sides (the $\Lambda^0$ has $S = -1$, the $K^0$ has $S = +1$).

The reaction is allowed by the strong interaction because all the quantum numbers are conserved.

### Example 2 — Threshold energy for $\pi^-$ production in $p + p$ collisions

Estimate the threshold lab energy for producing a $\pi^0$ in $p + p \to p + p + \pi^0$. The proton mass is 938 MeV and the pion mass is 135 MeV.

**Solution.** The Q-value of the reaction (in the centre-of-mass frame) is $Q = m_\pi c^2 = 135$ MeV. The threshold lab energy is

$$T_{\text{thr}} = Q \frac{m_p + m_p + m_\pi}{2 m_p} = 135 \times \frac{2 \times 938 + 135}{2 \times 938} = 135 \times 1.072 = 145\,\text{MeV}.$$

The factor 1.072 is the recoil correction; it is small because the pion mass is much less than the proton mass.

### Example 3 — Lifetime of the muon

The muon decays via $\mu^- \to e^- + \bar{\nu}_e + \nu_\mu$ with a mean life of $2.2\,\mu$s. The pion decays via $\pi^- \to \mu^- + \bar{\nu}_\mu$ with a mean life of 26 ns. Why is the muon lifetime so much longer than the pion lifetime, even though both are weak decays?

**Solution.** Both are weak decays, but the phase space is very different. The pion decay is a two-body decay; the muon carries away a fixed kinetic energy of about 30 MeV, and the neutrino is monoenergetic. The decay rate is proportional to the square of the matrix element times the phase-space factor.

The muon decay is a three-body decay; the energy is shared among the electron, the two neutrinos, and the muon recoil. The phase space is much larger, and the matrix element is comparable. The result is a much longer lifetime. The ratio of lifetimes is approximately

$$\frac{\tau_\mu}{\tau_\pi} \approx \left(\frac{m_\mu}{m_\pi}\right)^5 \left(\frac{f_\pi}{f_\mu}\right)^2,$$

which is of order $10^4$ to $10^5$ for the observed ratio of about $10^2$. The exact value depends on the matrix elements.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Quarks have been observed as free particles."** No. Quarks are confined inside hadrons; they have never been observed as free particles. The evidence for quarks is indirect, from deep inelastic scattering and from the spectroscopy of hadrons.
- **"The Standard Model explains everything."** It explains all known particles and three of the four forces to high precision, but it does not include gravity, does not explain the masses of the fermions (they are free parameters), and has no dark matter candidate.
- **"The Higgs boson gives mass to everything."** The Higgs gives mass to the $W$ and $Z$ bosons and to the fermions via Yukawa couplings. The mass of the proton is almost entirely binding energy of the strong force, not Higgs.
- **"Antimatter is exotic."** Antimatter is produced routinely in radioactive decays, in cosmic-ray interactions, and in accelerators. The positron was the first antimatter particle discovered (1932). The puzzle is why the universe has so little antimatter.
- **"Particle physics is unrelated to nuclear physics."** Nuclear physics is a low-energy manifestation of the strong and electroweak forces; the same quarks and gluons that make up hadrons make up nuclei. The techniques of particle physics (QCD lattice calculations, effective field theories) are increasingly used in nuclear physics.

## Connections

- The Standard Model is the framework for understanding all of nuclear physics; the quarks inside nucleons and the gluons that bind them determine the structure of nuclei.
- The weak interaction is responsible for beta decay and for the slow step of the pp-chain in stars; without the weak interaction, the sun would not shine.
- The strong interaction is responsible for nuclear binding, for the existence of hadrons, and for most of the mass of ordinary matter.
- Neutrino oscillations, observed in solar and atmospheric neutrinos, require neutrinos to have mass; this is the first physics beyond the original Standard Model.
- The dark matter problem connects particle physics to cosmology; the dark matter particle (if it exists) is a new particle beyond the Standard Model.

## Takeaway

- The Standard Model classifies all known particles into six quarks, six leptons, and four gauge bosons, plus the Higgs boson.
- Three of the four fundamental forces — electromagnetic, weak, and strong — are described by gauge theories unified in the Standard Model.
- The Higgs mechanism gives mass to the $W$ and $Z$ bosons and to the fermions.
- Conservation laws (charge, baryon number, lepton number, strangeness, parity) govern particle interactions.
- Open questions — neutrino masses, dark matter, dark energy, matter–antimatter asymmetry, quantum gravity — drive current research.
