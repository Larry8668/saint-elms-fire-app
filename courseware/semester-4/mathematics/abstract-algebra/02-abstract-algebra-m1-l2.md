# Examples of Groups — Z, R*, GL(n)

## Overview
Groups appear everywhere in mathematics, often in disguise. This lesson works through the most important examples: the integers under addition, the non-zero reals under multiplication, the general linear group, the symmetric group, the cyclic groups, and the dihedral groups. The goal is fluency in recognising groups, verifying the axioms, and computing with the standard examples.

## Learning Path
- What you should already know: the group axioms, basic set theory, matrix multiplication, permutations.
- What this lesson adds: a working knowledge of the standard groups, with examples and computations.
- What it unlocks: subgroups, homomorphisms, quotient groups, and the symmetry groups of physics.

## Core Explanation
**The integers under addition.** $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ is an abelian group. Identity $0$. Inverse $-n$. Every subgroup is of the form $n\mathbb{Z} = \{nk : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ for some non-negative integer $n$. (This is the only infinite cyclic group up to isomorphism.)

**The integers mod $n$.** $\mathbb{Z}_n = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, the integers modulo $n$, with addition mod $n$. Cyclic of order $n$, generated by $1$. Elements: $0, 1, \ldots, n - 1$. Every subgroup is cyclic; for each divisor $d$ of $n$, the unique subgroup of order $d$ is generated by $n/d$.

**Multiplicative groups of fields.** For a field $F$ (like $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$), $F^* = F \setminus \{0\}$ under multiplication is an abelian group. $\mathbb{R}^* = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$. $\mathbb{Q}^*$ similarly. $\mathbb{C}^*$ is the non-zero complex numbers under multiplication.

**Roots of unity.** In $\mathbb{C}^*$, the $n$-th roots of unity $\{\omega^k : k = 0, \ldots, n-1\}$ form a cyclic subgroup of order $n$, generated by $\omega = e^{2 \pi i/n}$. This is the group $C_n$ or $\mathbb{Z}_n$.

**General linear group GL$(n, F)$.** The set of $n \times n$ invertible matrices over a field $F$ (typically $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$) under matrix multiplication. The identity is the identity matrix $I$. The inverse is the matrix inverse. GL$(n, F)$ is a group; for $n \ge 2$ it is non-abelian.

**Special linear group SL$(n, F)$.** The subgroup of GL$(n, F)$ with determinant $1$. Important in physics for the volume-preserving linear transformations.

**Orthogonal group O$(n)$.** Subgroup of GL$(n, \mathbb{R})$ with $A^T A = I$. The elements are the distance-preserving linear maps (rotations and reflections). SO$(n) = $ O$(n) \cap$ SL$(n, \mathbb{R})$ is the rotation group in $n$ dimensions.

**Unitary group U$(n)$.** Subgroup of GL$(n, \mathbb{C})$ with $A^\dagger A = I$ (preserves the complex inner product). Important in quantum mechanics (preserves probabilities) and particle physics.

**Symmetric group $S_n$.** The set of all permutations of $n$ objects, under composition. Has order $n!$. $S_1, S_2, S_3, S_4, \ldots$. Every finite group is a subgroup of some $S_n$ (Cayley's theorem).

**Permutation notation.** A permutation $\sigma$ is a bijection from $\{1, \ldots, n\}$ to itself. Written in cycle notation, e.g. $(1 2 3)$ means $1 \to 2, 2 \to 3, 3 \to 1$. Disjoint cycles commute. The order of a permutation is the lcm of the cycle lengths.

**Sign of a permutation.** $\text{sgn}(\sigma) = (-1)^{n - c}$, where $c$ is the number of cycles (including fixed points) in the cycle decomposition. Even permutations have sign $+1$, odd have sign $-1$. $A_n$ is the alternating subgroup of even permutations, of order $n!/2$.

**Transposition.** A 2-cycle, e.g. $(i j)$. Every permutation is a product of transpositions. The parity (sign) is the parity of the number of transpositions.

**Cayley's theorem.** Every group $G$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $S_G$ (the symmetric group on the set $G$ itself). The isomorphism sends $g$ to the permutation $\lambda_g(x) = g * x$. This is why permutation groups are the universal finite groups.

**Dihedral group $D_{2n}$.** Symmetries of a regular $n$-gon. Has $2n$ elements: $n$ rotations and $n$ reflections. Generated by a rotation $r$ of order $n$ and a reflection $s$ of order $2$, with $srs = r^{-1}$. The dihedral group is non-abelian for $n \ge 3$.

**Quaternion group $Q_8$.** $\{\pm 1, \pm i, \pm j, \pm k\}$ with $i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = -1$ and $ij = k, jk = i, ki = j$. Order $8$, non-abelian. Every subgroup is normal.

**Direct product.** $G \times H$ is the set of pairs with componentwise operation. The order is $|G| \times |H|$. $\mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2$ has order $4$ but is not cyclic (no element of order $4$). This is the Klein four-group $V_4$.

**Recognising groups.** Any set closed under an associative operation with identity and inverses is a group. Examples: roots of unity under multiplication, rotations of a regular polygon, matrices of determinant $1$ under multiplication, polynomial functions on a finite field, etc.

**Group homomorphisms (preview).** A map $\phi: G \to H$ is a homomorphism if $\phi(g_1 g_2) = \phi(g_1) \phi(g_2)$. The image $\phi(G)$ is a subgroup of $H$; the kernel $\phi^{-1}(e)$ is a subgroup of $G$. The First Isomorphism Theorem: $G / \ker\phi \cong \phi(G)$.

## Key Ideas
- $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$: integers, abelian, infinite cyclic.
- $\mathbb{Z}_n$: cyclic of order $n$, generated by $1$.
- $\mathbb{R}^*$: non-zero reals, abelian.
- GL$(n, F)$: invertible matrices, non-abelian for $n \ge 2$.
- $S_n$: permutations, $n!$ elements.
- $D_{2n}$: symmetries of a regular $n$-gon, $2n$ elements.

## Worked Examples
**Example 1 — Inverse in $\mathbb{Z}_{11}$.** $7 \cdot 8 = 56 = 5 \cdot 11 + 1$, so $7 \cdot 8 = 1 \pmod{11}$. Hence $7^{-1} = 8$ in $\mathbb{Z}_{11}$.

**Example 2 — Order of a permutation.** $\sigma = (1 2 3)(4 5)$ in $S_5$. Cycle lengths $3$ and $2$, so order $= \text{lcm}(3, 2) = 6$. ✓

**Example 3 — GL$(2, \mathbb{R})$.** The matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $1$, so it is in GL$(2, \mathbb{R})$. Its inverse is $A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ (a shear in the opposite direction).

**Example 4 — Dihedral group $D_6$.** Symmetries of a regular hexagon. 6 rotations (by $60°$ multiples) and 6 reflections (3 through opposite vertices, 3 through midpoints of opposite edges). $r^6 = e$, $s^2 = e$, $srs = r^{-1}$.

## Common Misconceptions
- **"$\mathbb{Z}_n$ is the same as $\{0, 1, \ldots, n-1\}$."** It is more than that — the operation is addition mod $n$, and the equivalence is on integers.
- **"Every subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$ is $\mathbb{Z}$ itself."** No — $n\mathbb{Z}$ for $n \ge 1$ is a subgroup, and there are infinitely many.
- **"All $n \times n$ matrices form a group."** No — singular matrices have no inverse. The group is GL$(n)$.
- **"A permutation is a transposition."** No — a permutation is a general bijection. A transposition is a 2-cycle. Every permutation is a product of transpositions, but is not itself one (except in special cases).

## Connections
The symmetric group $S_n$ classifies the permutations of $n$ objects and underlies the combinatorics of many counting problems. The general linear group GL$(n)$ and its subgroups (SL, O, U, SU) are the symmetry groups of classical and quantum physics. The dihedral groups are the prototypes of point groups in crystallography. Cyclic groups describe the discrete rotational symmetries of polygons and the time-evolution of periodic systems.

- GL$(n, F)$: invertible $n \times n$ matrices.
