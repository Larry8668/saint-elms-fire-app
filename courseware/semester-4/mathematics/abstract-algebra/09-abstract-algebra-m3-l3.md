# Fields, Field Extensions and Finite Fields

## Overview
A *field* is a commutative ring in which every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse: a number system where you can do all of arithmetic, including division. The rationals $\mathbb{Q}$, the reals $\mathbb{R}$, and the complexes $\mathbb{C}$ are fields. Finite fields $\mathbb{F}_q$ are also important — they are the building blocks of coding theory, cryptography, and finite geometry. This lesson develops the basic theory of fields: subfields, extensions, algebraic and transcendental elements, the degree of an extension, and the structure of finite fields.

## Learning Path
- What you should already know: rings, ideals, integral domains, the polynomial ring $F[x]$.
- What this lesson adds: fields, field extensions, algebraic and transcendental elements, finite fields.
- What it unlocks: Galois theory, algebraic number theory, coding theory, and the mathematics of cryptography.

## Core Explanation
**Field axioms.** A *field* $F$ is a commutative ring with unity $1 \ne 0$ in which every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse. Equivalently:
- $(F, +)$ is an abelian group.
- $(F \setminus \{0\}, \times)$ is an abelian group.
- Distributive law: $a (b + c) = a b + a c$.

**Examples of fields.**
- $\mathbb{Q}$: rationals.
- $\mathbb{R}$: reals.
- $\mathbb{C}$: complexes.
- $\mathbb{F}_p = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$: finite field of order $p$, for $p$ prime.
- $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$: finite field of order $p^n$ (for any prime $p$ and $n \ge 1$).
- $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}) = \{a + b \sqrt{2} : a, b \in \mathbb{Q}\}$: a quadratic extension.
- $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ for any algebraic $\alpha$: a finite extension.

**Non-examples.**
- $\mathbb{Z}$: integers, no multiplicative inverses (only $\pm 1$).
- $\mathbb{Z}_n$ for $n$ composite: zero-divisors, not a domain.
- Polynomial ring $F[x]$: $x$ has no inverse.

**Subfield.** A subfield of $F$ is a subset closed under the field operations, containing $0$ and $1$, and containing inverses.

**Examples of subfields.** $\mathbb{Q}$ is a subfield of $\mathbb{R}$ of $\mathbb{C}$. The prime subfield of a field of characteristic $p$ is $\mathbb{F}_p$; of a field of characteristic $0$ is $\mathbb{Q}$.

**Characteristic.** The smallest positive integer $n$ such that $n \cdot 1 = 0$, or $0$ if no such $n$. Fields have characteristic $0$ (containing $\mathbb{Q}$) or characteristic $p$ (containing $\mathbb{F}_p$).

**Field extension.** A *field extension* $E/F$ is a field $E$ containing a subfield isomorphic to $F$. Equivalently, $E$ is a field and $F$ is a subfield.

**Examples of extensions.** $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})/\mathbb{Q}$.

**Degree of an extension.** For a finite extension $E/F$, the degree is $[E : F] = \dim_F E$ (dimension of $E$ as a vector space over $F$). Examples: $[\mathbb{C} : \mathbb{R}] = 2$ (basis $\{1, i\}$), $[\mathbb{R} : \mathbb{Q}] = \infty$ (uncountable basis), $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}) : \mathbb{Q}] = 2$ (basis $\{1, \sqrt{2}\}$).

**Tower law.** If $E/F$ and $F/K$ are finite extensions, then $E/K$ is finite and $[E : K] = [E : F] [F : K]$.

**Algebraic elements.** An element $\alpha \in E$ is *algebraic* over $F$ if $\alpha$ is a root of a non-zero polynomial in $F[x]$. Equivalently, the evaluation map $F[x] \to E$ (sending $x$ to $\alpha$) has a non-trivial kernel, which is a maximal ideal (since $F[x]$ is a PID), generated by an irreducible polynomial $m_\alpha(x) \in F[x]$, the *minimal polynomial* of $\alpha$.

**Transcendental elements.** $\alpha \in E$ is *transcendental* over $F$ if it is not algebraic — it does not satisfy any non-zero polynomial over $F$. Example: $\pi$ and $e$ are believed to be transcendental over $\mathbb{Q}$ (this is hard to prove for some constants).

**Simple extensions.** If $\alpha$ is algebraic over $F$, then $F(\alpha) \cong F[x]/(m_\alpha(x))$, with degree $[F(\alpha) : F] = \deg m_\alpha$. If $\alpha$ is transcendental, $F(\alpha) \cong F(x)$, the rational function field in one variable.

**Algebraic closure.** A field $F$ is *algebraically closed* if every non-constant polynomial in $F[x]$ has a root in $F$. The algebraic closure $\overline{F}$ is a field extension of $F$ that is algebraically closed and algebraic over $F$. Examples: $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ (algebraic numbers), $\overline{\mathbb{F}_p} = \bigcup_n \mathbb{F}_{p^n}$.

**Steinitz's theorem.** Every field has an algebraic closure, unique up to isomorphism.

**Fundamental theorem of algebra.** $\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed.

**Splitting field.** For a polynomial $f \in F[x]$, the *splitting field* is the smallest extension of $F$ over which $f$ factors into linear factors. Always exists, unique up to isomorphism.

**Examples of splitting fields.**
- $x^2 + 1$ over $\mathbb{R}$: splitting field is $\mathbb{C}$ (or $\mathbb{R}[i]$).
- $x^3 - 2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$: splitting field is $\mathbb{Q}(\root 3 \of 2, \omega)$ where $\omega = e^{2 \pi i / 3}$, with degree $6$.
- $x^p - x$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$: splitting field is $\mathbb{F}_p$ itself (the polynomial has all its roots in $\mathbb{F}_p$).

**Normal extensions.** An algebraic extension is *normal* if it is the splitting field of some polynomial. Equivalently, every irreducible polynomial in $F[x]$ with a root in $E$ splits completely in $E$.

**Separable extensions.** An algebraic extension is *separable* if every element's minimal polynomial has distinct roots (in the algebraic closure). In characteristic $0$, every algebraic extension is separable. In characteristic $p$, inseparable extensions can occur (e.g. $\mathbb{F}_p(t^{1/p})/\mathbb{F}_p(t)$).

**Galois extensions.** A *Galois extension* is one that is both normal and separable. The group of $F$-automorphisms of $E$ is the *Galois group* $\text{Gal}(E/F)$. The order of the Galois group equals the degree: $|\text{Gal}(E/F)| = [E : F]$.

**Examples of Galois groups.**
- $\text{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})/\mathbb{Q}) = \mathbb{Z}_2$: complex conjugation on $\sqrt{2}$ (or its negation).
- $\text{Gal}(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}) = \mathbb{Z}_2$: complex conjugation.
- $\text{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(e^{2 \pi i/n})/\mathbb{Q}) = (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^*$: units mod $n$.

**Fundamental theorem of Galois theory.** A bijection between subgroups of $\text{Gal}(E/F)$ and intermediate fields $F \subseteq K \subseteq E$, with the inclusion-reversing property. Normal subgroups correspond to normal extensions; subgroups of index $n$ correspond to extensions of degree $n$. This is the deep theorem of Galois theory.

**Finite fields.** A finite field $\mathbb{F}_q$ of order $q = p^n$ exists for every prime $p$ and positive integer $n$, and is unique up to isomorphism. Its multiplicative group $\mathbb{F}_q^*$ is cyclic of order $q - 1$.

**Construction of finite fields.** $\mathbb{F}_{p^n} \cong \mathbb{F}_p[x]/(f(x))$, where $f$ is an irreducible polynomial of degree $n$ in $\mathbb{F}_p[x]$. For example, $\mathbb{F}_4 = \mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x^2 + x + 1)$.

**Automorphism group of $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$.** $\text{Gal}(\mathbb{F}_{p^n}/\mathbb{F}_p) = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, generated by the Frobenius automorphism $\phi(x) = x^p$. (Always a cyclic group of order $n$.)

**Trace and norm.** For $\alpha \in \mathbb{F}_{p^n}$, the trace is $T(\alpha) = \alpha + \alpha^p + \alpha^{p^2} + \cdots + \alpha^{p^{n-1}} \in \mathbb{F}_p$, and the norm is $N(\alpha) = \alpha \cdot \alpha^p \cdots \alpha^{p^{n-1}} = \alpha^{(p^n - 1)/(p - 1)} \in \mathbb{F}_p$. The norm is a unit iff $\alpha \ne 0$.

**Applications of finite fields.** Coding theory: Reed–Solomon codes, BCH codes, and many others are constructed over finite fields. Cryptography: AES uses $\mathbb{F}_{2^8}$, elliptic-curve cryptography uses finite fields. Combinatorics: projective planes over $\mathbb{F}_q$ exist for $q$ a prime power. Number theory: Hasse–Weil zeta functions of curves over $\mathbb{F}_q$.

**Field of fractions.** Any integral domain $R$ has a smallest field containing it, the field of fractions. For $R = \mathbb{Z}$, this is $\mathbb{Q}$. For $R = k[x]$, this is $k(x)$, the rational function field. For $R = \mathcal{O}_K$ (ring of integers of a number field), this is $K$ itself.

**Transcendental extensions.** A purely transcendental extension $k(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ is the field of rational functions in $n$ variables. Every field extension is a transcendental extension of some subfield, followed by an algebraic extension.

**Simple vs. non-simple extensions.** Not every extension is simple (generated by a single element). For example, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3})$ is simple (this is the primitive element theorem, valid for finite separable extensions in characteristic $0$). But $\mathbb{F}_p(x, y)$ may not be simple over $\mathbb{F}_p(x^p, y^p)$ in characteristic $p$.

**Primitive element theorem.** Every finite separable extension of a field $F$ of characteristic $0$ is simple: $E = F(\alpha)$ for some $\alpha \in E$. (The result is more subtle in characteristic $p$.)

**Minimal polynomial.** For $\alpha$ algebraic over $F$, the minimal polynomial $m_\alpha(x) \in F[x]$ is the unique monic irreducible polynomial with $\alpha$ as a root. Its degree is $[F(\alpha) : F]$.

**Algebraic numbers.** $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ is the field of all complex numbers algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}$. It is countable, dense in $\mathbb{C}$, and Galois over $\mathbb{Q}$. The Galois group $\text{Gal}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q})$ is a profinite group, fundamental in number theory.

**Transcendental number theory.** Lindemann (1882) proved that $\pi$ and $e$ are transcendental. Gelfond–Schneider theorem: if $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are algebraic with $\alpha \ne 0, 1$ and $\beta$ irrational, then $\alpha^\beta$ is transcendental. The smallest unknown cases ($e + \pi$, $e \pi$, etc.) remain open.

**Field extensions in physics.** The complexification of a real field appears everywhere: complex analysis in quantum mechanics, complex projective spaces in twistor theory, complex Lie algebras in particle physics. Finite fields appear in quantum error correction, topological quantum computing, and lattice gauge theory.

**Symbolic computation.** Computer algebra systems (Mathematica, Maple, Sage) implement field arithmetic over $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{F}_p$, $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$, algebraic extensions. The field of rational functions $k(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ and the polynomial ring $k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ are basic data structures.

## Key Ideas
- A field is a commutative ring with unity where every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse.
- Field extension $E/F$ has degree $[E : F] = \dim_F E$.
- An element is algebraic over $F$ if it is a root of a polynomial in $F[x]$; otherwise transcendental.
- Finite fields $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ exist for all $p$ prime, $n \ge 1$, and are unique up to isomorphism.
- Galois theory: bijection between subgroups of $\text{Gal}(E/F)$ and intermediate fields.

## Worked Examples
**Example 1 — Find the degree of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.** $\sqrt[3]{2}$ is a root of $x^3 - 2 \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$. By Eisenstein, $x^3 - 2$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$. So $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}) = \mathbb{Q}[x]/(x^3 - 2)$, with degree $3$. A basis: $\{1, \sqrt[3]{2}, \sqrt[3]{4}\}$.

**Example 2 — Construct $\mathbb{F}_8$.** $\mathbb{F}_8 = \mathbb{F}_2[x]/(f(x))$, where $f$ is an irreducible cubic over $\mathbb{F}_2$. Try $x^3 + x + 1$ (no roots in $\mathbb{F}_2$: $0^3 + 0 + 1 = 1$, $1^3 + 1 + 1 = 1$, both $\ne 0$). So $x^3 + x + 1$ is irreducible, and $\mathbb{F}_8 = \mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x^3 + x + 1)$. Elements: $\{0, 1, x, x+1, x^2, x^2+1, x^2+x, x^2+x+1\}$ — eight elements.

**Example 3 — Splitting field of $x^4 - 5$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.** Roots: $\pm 5^{1/4}, \pm i \cdot 5^{1/4}$. Splitting field: $\mathbb{Q}(5^{1/4}, i)$. Degree: $[\mathbb{Q}(5^{1/4}) : \mathbb{Q}] = 4$ (since $x^4 - 5$ is irreducible). $[\mathbb{Q}(5^{1/4}, i) : \mathbb{Q}(5^{1/4})] = 2$ (adjoining $i$). Total: $[\mathbb{Q}(5^{1/4}, i) : \mathbb{Q}] = 8$.

**Example 4 — Trace in $\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$.** For $\alpha \in \mathbb{F}_{p^2}$, $T(\alpha) = \alpha + \alpha^p \in \mathbb{F}_p$. Take $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ (so $x^2 + 1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{F}_p$) and write $\mathbb{F}_{p^2} = \mathbb{F}_p[i]$ with $i^2 = -1$. Then $\alpha = a + b i$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{F}_p$, and $\alpha^p = a^p + b^p i^p = a + b i^p$. Now $i^p = i (i^2)^{(p-1)/2} = i (-1)^{(p-1)/2} = -i$ (since $(p-1)/2$ is odd for $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$). So $T(\alpha) = (a + bi) + (a - bi) = 2 a \in \mathbb{F}_p$. For $p = 3$: $\mathbb{F}_9 = \mathbb{F}_3[i]/(i^2 + 1)$, $i^3 = -i$, $T(a + bi) = 2a$. (For $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, $x^2 + 1$ splits over $\mathbb{F}_p$, so this representation does not define a quadratic extension; use a different irreducible quadratic. For $p = 2$, $x^2 + 1 = (x+1)^2$ is not irreducible; use $x^2 + x + 1$.)

## Common Misconceptions
- **"$\mathbb{R}$ is the largest field."** No — $\mathbb{C}$ is bigger. And neither is algebraically closed (well, $\mathbb{C}$ is, by the fundamental theorem of algebra).
- **"$\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ is countable."** It is — but it's dense in $\mathbb{C}$, which is uncountable.
- **"All field extensions are simple."** Not in characteristic $p$ (e.g. $\mathbb{F}_p(x, y)/\mathbb{F}_p(x^p, y^p)$ is not simple).
- **"Finite fields exist only for prime $p$."** No — $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ exists for any prime $p$ and any $n \ge 1$.

## Connections
Field extensions and Galois theory are the foundations of algebraic number theory, algebraic geometry, and the theory of equations. Finite fields underlie coding theory, cryptography, and finite geometry. The complexification of real numbers is essential in analysis, and the algebraic closure of the rationals is fundamental in number theory.

## Takeaway
- A field is a ring with division.
- Field extension $E/F$ has degree $[E : F]$.
- Algebraic over $F$ if a root of a polynomial in $F[x]$; transcendental otherwise.
- Finite fields $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ exist for all prime $p$, $n \ge 1$.
- Galois theory: subgroups of $\text{Gal}(E/F)$ ↔ intermediate fields.
