# Ideals, Quotient Rings and Homomorphisms

## Overview
An *ideal* of a ring $R$ is a special kind of subring — closed under multiplication by any element of $R$, not just its own elements. Ideals are to rings what normal subgroups are to groups: they are the kernels of homomorphisms, the building blocks of quotient rings, and the local structure of the ring (via maximal and prime ideals). This lesson develops the theory, with examples from $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, and matrix rings.

## Learning Path
- What you should already know: rings, homomorphisms, subrings, units, fields.
- What this lesson adds: ideals, quotient rings, the isomorphism theorems, prime and maximal ideals.
- What it unlocks: algebraic number theory, algebraic geometry, and the theory of modules.

## Core Explanation
**Ideal definition.** A subset $I \subseteq R$ is an *ideal* of $R$ if:
1. $(I, +)$ is a subgroup of $(R, +)$: $0 \in I$, closed under addition and negation.
2. Absorption: $r i \in I$ and $i r \in I$ for all $r \in R$, $i \in I$. (If $R$ is commutative, just $r i \in I$.)

**Two-sided ideal.** A subset closed under both left and right multiplication. For a commutative ring, every ideal is two-sided.

**Examples of ideals.**
- In $\mathbb{Z}$: every ideal is $n\mathbb{Z}$ for some $n \ge 0$. (Generated by a single integer $n$.)
- In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$: $(2, x) = \{2 f + x g : f, g \in \mathbb{Z}[x]\}$ — the ideal generated by $2$ and $x$.
- In $M_n(R)$: ideals are $M_n(I)$ for $I$ an ideal of $R$.
- $\{0\}$ and $R$ are always ideals.

**Principal ideal.** An ideal generated by a single element: $(a) = \{r a : r \in R\}$. In $\mathbb{Z}$, every ideal is principal. In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, some ideals are not principal — e.g. $(2, x)$ is not principal (you cannot write a single generator for it).

**Quotient ring.** For an ideal $I$, the cosets $r + I = \{r + i : i \in I\}$ partition $R$. The set of cosets $R/I$ is a ring under $(r + I)(s + I) = rs + I$. The zero is $0 + I = I$; the unity (if $R$ has one) is $1 + I$.

**Examples of quotient rings.**
- $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} = \mathbb{Z}_n$ for any $n$.
- $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(2) \cong \mathbb{F}_2[x]$ (polynomials over $\mathbb{F}_2$).
- $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ (modding out by $x$ sets $x = 0$).
- $\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2 + 1) \cong \mathbb{C}$ (modding out by $x^2 + 1$ identifies $x$ with $i$).
- $\mathbb{Q}[x]/(x^2 - 2) \cong \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ (a quadratic field).

**Ring homomorphisms.** A map $\phi: R \to S$ is a ring homomorphism if $\phi(a + b) = \phi(a) + \phi(b)$ and $\phi(a b) = \phi(a) \phi(b)$. (Sometimes also $\phi(1) = 1$ for unital homomorphisms; the convention varies.)

**Kernel of a ring homomorphism.** $\ker \phi = \{r \in R : \phi(r) = 0_S\}$ is an ideal of $R$. (Verification: $0 \in \ker\phi$; closed under addition and negation; if $i \in \ker\phi$ and $r \in R$, then $\phi(r i) = \phi(r) \phi(i) = \phi(r) \cdot 0 = 0$, so $r i \in \ker\phi$.)

**Image of a ring homomorphism.** $\text{im}(\phi) = \{\phi(r) : r \in R\}$ is a subring of $S$.

**First isomorphism theorem for rings.** If $\phi: R \to S$ is a ring homomorphism, then $R/\ker\phi \cong \text{im}(\phi)$. Proof: the map $r + \ker\phi \mapsto \phi(r)$ is a well-defined, bijective ring homomorphism.

**Examples of the first isomorphism theorem.**
- $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}_n$, $k \mapsto k \mod n$: kernel $n\mathbb{Z}$, image $\mathbb{Z}_n$. So $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}_n$.
- $\mathbb{Z}[x] \to \mathbb{Z}$, $f \mapsto f(0)$: kernel $(x)$, image $\mathbb{Z}$. So $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x) \cong \mathbb{Z}$.
- $\mathbb{R}[x] \to \mathbb{C}$, $f \mapsto f(i)$: kernel $(x^2 + 1)$, image $\mathbb{C}$. So $\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2 + 1) \cong \mathbb{C}$.

**Second and third isomorphism theorems for rings.** The same as for groups, with ideals in place of normal subgroups:
- Second: if $I$ is an ideal of $R$ and $S$ is a subring, then $S/(S \cap I) \cong (S + I)/I$.
- Third: if $I \subseteq J$ are ideals of $R$, then $J/I$ is an ideal of $R/I$, and $(R/I)/(J/I) \cong R/J$.

**Prime ideals.** An ideal $P \ne R$ is *prime* if for all $a, b \in R$, $a b \in P$ implies $a \in P$ or $b \in P$. Equivalently, $R/P$ is an integral domain.

**Examples of prime ideals.**
- $(0)$ is prime in an integral domain.
- $(p)$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ for $p$ prime: $\mathbb{Z}/(p) = \mathbb{F}_p$, a field, hence an integral domain.
- $(x)$ in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$: $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x) \cong \mathbb{Z}$, an integral domain.
- $(x^2 + 1)$ in $\mathbb{R}[x]$: $\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2 + 1) \cong \mathbb{C}$, an integral domain.

**Maximal ideals.** An ideal $M \ne R$ is *maximal* if there is no ideal strictly between $M$ and $R$. Equivalently, $R/M$ is a field.

**Examples of maximal ideals.**
- $(p)$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ for $p$ prime: maximal. $\mathbb{Z}/(p) = \mathbb{F}_p$, a field.
- $(x)$ in $\mathbb{F}_2[x]$: maximal. $\mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x) \cong \mathbb{F}_2$.
- $(x, 2)$ in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$: maximal. $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x, 2) \cong \mathbb{F}_2$.

**Maximal implies prime.** Every maximal ideal is prime. The converse is false: $(0)$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ is prime but not maximal.

**Existence of maximal ideals.** Every non-unit proper ideal is contained in a maximal ideal (Zorn's lemma / axiom of choice). This is the algebraic form of the fundamental existence theorem.

**Quotient by maximal ideal is a field.** If $M$ is maximal in $R$, then $R/M$ is a field. Conversely, if $R/I$ is a field, then $I$ is maximal.

**Chinese remainder theorem.** If $I, J$ are comaximal ideals ($I + J = R$), then $R/(I \cap J) \cong R/I \times R/J$. The map $r \mapsto (r + I, r + J)$ is the isomorphism. For example, $\mathbb{Z}/15 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3 \times \mathbb{Z}/5$ (since $3\mathbb{Z} + 5\mathbb{Z} = \mathbb{Z}$).

**Generalised Chinese remainder theorem.** If $I_1, \ldots, I_n$ are pairwise comaximal, then $R/(I_1 \cap \cdots \cap I_n) \cong R/I_1 \times \cdots \times R/I_n$. Useful in number theory for the structure of $\mathbb{Z}/n$.

**Prime spectrum.** The set of all prime ideals of a ring $R$, denoted $\text{Spec}(R)$, is a topological space (with the Zariski topology). This is the foundation of algebraic geometry: each prime ideal is a "point" of an affine variety.

**Nilradical.** The set of nilpotent elements of $R$ (elements $a$ with $a^n = 0$ for some $n$) forms an ideal, the nilradical $\text{nil}(R)$. It is the intersection of all prime ideals.

**Jacobson radical.** The intersection of all maximal ideals. The Jacobson radical contains the nilradical, and they are equal in many important rings.

**Operations on ideals.** For ideals $I, J$ of $R$:
- Sum: $I + J = \{i + j : i \in I, j \in J\}$ — the smallest ideal containing $I$ and $J$.
- Intersection: $I \cap J$.
- Product: $I J$ — the ideal generated by $\{i j : i \in I, j \in J\}$.
- Radical: $\sqrt{I} = \{r \in R : r^n \in I \text{ for some } n\}$.

**Prime avoidance lemma.** If $I \subseteq P_1 \cup \cdots \cup P_n$ where $P_i$ are prime ideals and $I$ is an ideal, then $I \subseteq P_i$ for some $i$. Useful for "prime avoidance" arguments.

**Nakayama's lemma.** If $M$ is a finitely generated module over a ring $R$ and $J$ is an ideal contained in the Jacobson radical, and $J M = M$, then $M = 0$. (Specialised: if $M$ is finitely generated and $J$ contains a unit, then $M = 0$.)

**Localisation.** For a multiplicative set $S$ in $R$, the localisation $S^{-1} R$ is a ring of fractions with elements $r/s$ for $r \in R, s \in S$. The prime ideals of $S^{-1} R$ correspond to the primes of $R$ disjoint from $S$. Localisation is the algebraic analog of "looking near a point".

**Examples of ideals in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$.**
- $(0)$: prime (since $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is an integral domain), not maximal.
- $(p)$ for $p$ prime: prime, but $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(p) = \mathbb{F}_p[x]$ is an integral domain, not a field, so not maximal.
- $(p, f(x))$ where $f$ is irreducible mod $p$: maximal, by the same argument.
- $(2, x)$: maximal, $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(2, x) \cong \mathbb{F}_2$.

**Ideals in physics.** The space of ideals of a ring corresponds to the spectrum, the "space of values" of the ring. In quantum mechanics, ideals of an operator algebra correspond to subspaces invariant under the operators. In gauge theory, ideals of the algebra of observables correspond to the constraints of the theory.

**Dimension theory.** The *Krull dimension* of a ring is the maximum length of a chain of prime ideals. For $\mathbb{Z}$: dimension $1$ (chain $(0) \subset (p)$). For $\mathbb{Z}[x]$: dimension $2$ (chain $(0) \subset (p) \subset (p, x)$). For $k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$: dimension $n$.

**Affine varieties.** The set of common zeros of a set of polynomials in $\overline{k}^n$ is an *affine algebraic variety*. The polynomial ring $k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]/I$ (where $I$ is the ideal of relations) is the *coordinate ring* of the variety. The Zariski topology is defined by zero sets of ideals. This is the foundation of algebraic geometry.

**Why ideals are not just subrings.** A subring closed under addition is closed under subtraction (since $-a = -1 \cdot a$, but $-1$ may not be in the subring). An ideal is closed under multiplication by any element of $R$, including $-1$, so it is automatically closed under negation. The absorption property is the key.

## Key Ideas
- An ideal absorbs multiplication by $R$.
- Quotient ring $R/I$ is a ring.
- First iso theorem: $R/\ker\phi \cong \text{im}(\phi)$.
- $P$ is prime iff $R/P$ is an integral domain.
- $M$ is maximal iff $R/M$ is a field.
- Maximal implies prime, not conversely.

## Worked Examples
**Example 1 — Ideals of $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$.** Ideals correspond to divisors of $12$. The proper non-trivial ideals are $(2) = \{0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10\}$, $(3) = \{0, 3, 6, 9\}$, $(4) = \{0, 4, 8\}$, and $(6) = \{0, 6\}$. The quotient rings have orders $|\mathbb{Z}_{12}/(2)| = 2$, $|\mathbb{Z}_{12}/(3)| = 3$, $|\mathbb{Z}_{12}/(4)| = 4$, $|\mathbb{Z}_{12}/(6)| = 6$, so $\mathbb{Z}_{12}/(2) \cong \mathbb{Z}_2$ and $\mathbb{Z}_{12}/(3) \cong \mathbb{Z}_3$ (both fields), while $\mathbb{Z}_{12}/(4) \cong \mathbb{Z}_4$ and $\mathbb{Z}_{12}/(6) \cong \mathbb{Z}_6$ (neither a field nor a domain). Thus the **maximal** ideals are $(2)$ and $(3)$, and these are also the **prime** ideals (the other quotients have zero-divisors). Note $(4) \subsetneq (2) \subsetneq \mathbb{Z}_{12}$ and $(6) \subsetneq (2) \subsetneq \mathbb{Z}_{12}$, confirming $(4)$ and $(6)$ are not maximal. The zero ideal $(0)$ is not prime because $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ itself is not an integral domain.

**Example 2 — Maximal ideals of $\mathbb{Z}[x]$.** Examples: $(p, x)$ for any prime $p$, $(p, f(x))$ where $f$ is irreducible mod $p$. The maximal ideals correspond to "points" in the affine plane over a finite field.

**Example 3 — Quotient of polynomial ring.** $\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2 + 1)$: every coset has a unique representative $a + b x$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$. Multiplication: $(a + bx)(c + dx) = ac + (ad + bc) x + bd x^2 = ac - bd + (ad + bc) x$ (using $x^2 = -1$). This is exactly complex multiplication. So $\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2 + 1) \cong \mathbb{C}$.

**Example 4 — Chinese remainder theorem.** $\mathbb{Z}/15 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3 \times \mathbb{Z}/5$. The isomorphism sends $k \mapsto (k \mod 3, k \mod 5)$. The kernel is $\{k : k \equiv 0 \mod 3 \text{ and } k \equiv 0 \mod 5\} = 15\mathbb{Z}$.

## Common Misconceptions
- **"Every subring is an ideal."** No — an ideal is closed under multiplication by any element of $R$, not just its own.
- **"Prime and maximal are the same."** Maximal is stronger: every maximal ideal is prime, but not vice versa.
- **"Quotient by a subring is a ring."** Only by an ideal.
- **"$\mathbb{Z}_n$ is always a ring."** It is, but not always a domain or a field.

## Connections
Ideals are the foundation of algebraic number theory, algebraic geometry, and commutative algebra. The Chinese remainder theorem is the workhorse of computational number theory and of the design of error-correcting codes. Localisation is the language of "local" questions about rings (and is the foundation of scheme theory in algebraic geometry).

## Takeaway
- An ideal absorbs multiplication by the ring.
- $R/I$ is a ring under coset operations.
- $P$ prime iff $R/P$ is a domain; $M$ maximal iff $R/M$ is a field.
- Maximal $\Rightarrow$ prime.
- CRT: $R/(I \cap J) \cong R/I \times R/J$ for comaximal $I, J$.
