# Subgroups, Cyclic Groups and Generators

## Overview
A *subgroup* is a subset of a group that is itself a group under the same operation. The study of subgroups is central to group theory — it is how we dissect a large group into smaller, more manageable pieces. This lesson develops the subgroup test, characterises the subgroups of cyclic groups, introduces generators, and proves Lagrange's theorem, the first major theorem of finite group theory.

## Learning Path
- What you should already know: group axioms, permutations, the order of an element, the concept of a subgroup.
- What this lesson adds: the subgroup test, the structure of cyclic-group subgroups, generators, and Lagrange's theorem.
- What it unlocks: quotient groups, the isomorphism theorems, and the classification of finite groups.

## Core Explanation
**Subgroup definition.** A subset $H \subseteq G$ is a *subgroup* if, with the same operation, $H$ is a group. Trivial subgroups: $\{e\}$ and $G$ itself. A *proper* subgroup is one other than $G$.

**Subgroup test.** $H \subseteq G$ is a subgroup iff
1. $H$ is non-empty.
2. For all $a, b \in H$, $a b \in H$ (closure).
3. For all $a \in H$, $a^{-1} \in H$ (inverses).

A one-step version: $H$ is a subgroup iff $H$ is non-empty and for all $a, b \in H$, $a b^{-1} \in H$. (This combines closure and inverses.)

**Examples of subgroups.**
- $n\mathbb{Z} = \{nk : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$.
- The cyclic subgroup $\langle g \rangle = \{g^n : n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is a subgroup of any group $G$.
- SL$(n)$ is a subgroup of GL$(n)$.
- SO$(n)$ is a subgroup of O$(n)$, which is a subgroup of GL$(n, \mathbb{R})$.
- $A_n$ is a subgroup of $S_n$.

**Centre.** $Z(G) = \{g \in G : g h = h g \text{ for all } h \in G\}$. Always a subgroup (in fact, a normal subgroup).

**Generated subgroup.** For a subset $S \subseteq G$, the subgroup $\langle S \rangle$ generated by $S$ is the smallest subgroup of $G$ containing $S$. Equivalently, it is the set of all products of elements of $S$ and their inverses.

**Cyclic groups.** A group is *cyclic* if it is generated by a single element: $G = \langle g \rangle$ for some $g \in G$. Every cyclic group is abelian. Cyclic groups are classified: $G$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}$ (infinite) or $\mathbb{Z}_n$ (finite of order $n$).

**Order of an element.** The smallest positive integer $n$ such that $g^n = e$, if it exists. (For some elements, no such $n$ exists; then the order is infinite.) The element $g$ generates a cyclic subgroup of order equal to the order of $g$.

**Subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}_n$.** For each divisor $d$ of $n$, there is a unique subgroup of order $d$, generated by $n/d$. So the subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}_n$ are in bijection with the divisors of $n$.

**Subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}$.** Every subgroup is $n\mathbb{Z}$ for some $n \ge 0$. So the subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}$ are in bijection with the non-negative integers.

**Generators.** A set $S$ *generates* $G$ if $\langle S \rangle = G$. The cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}_n$ is generated by any $k$ with $\gcd(k, n) = 1$. The group $S_n$ is generated by the transposition $(1 2)$ and the $n$-cycle $(1 2 \ldots n)$.

**Lagrange's theorem.** The order of a subgroup $H$ divides the order of the group: $|H|$ divides $|G|$. The order of an element divides the order of the group.

**Proof of Lagrange.** Define an equivalence relation on $G$ by $g_1 \sim g_2$ iff $g_1 g_2^{-1} \in H$. The equivalence classes are the *left cosets* $g H = \{g h : h \in H\}$. They partition $G$, and each has $|H|$ elements. So $|G| = (\text{number of cosets}) \times |H|$.

**Index.** The *index* of $H$ in $G$ is $[G : H] = |G|/|H|$, the number of left cosets of $H$. Always a positive integer.

**Order of an element divides order of group.** The cyclic subgroup $\langle g \rangle$ has order equal to the order of $g$. By Lagrange, $|\langle g \rangle|$ divides $|G|$.

**Cyclic groups are abelian.** If $G = \langle g \rangle$, every element is $g^k$ for some $k$. Then $g^a g^b = g^{a + b} = g^b g^a$. ✓

**Fermat's little theorem (group form).** If $G$ is a finite group of order $n$, then $g^n = e$ for every $g \in G$. (Because the order of $g$ divides $n$.) For the multiplicative group $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^*$, this gives the classical Fermat's little theorem: $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$.

**Consequence of Lagrange.** A group of prime order is cyclic (and hence simple). $A_5$ has order $60$ (not prime), but it is the smallest non-abelian simple group.

**Subgroup lattice.** The set of all subgroups of $G$, ordered by inclusion, forms a *lattice*. For $S_3$: subgroups are $\{e\}$, three subgroups of order $2$ (each generated by a transposition), one of order $3$ (the 3-cycles), and $S_3$ itself.

**Centre of a group.** $|Z(G)|$ divides $|G|$; $Z(G)$ is a normal subgroup.

**Examples of group constructions.**
- Direct product $G \times H$: pairs $(g, h)$, componentwise operation.
- Direct sum $\bigoplus_i G_i$ (for finitely many $G_i$): same as direct product.
- Semi-direct product $G \rtimes_\phi H$: a more general construction where one group acts on another by automorphisms.
- Free group $F_S$ on a set $S$: words in the letters of $S$ and their inverses, with no relations.

**Subgroups and the structure theorem for finite abelian groups.** Every finite abelian group is a direct product of cyclic groups of prime-power order. The decomposition is unique up to reordering. This is the structure theorem for finite abelian groups, a major result of abstract algebra.

## Key Ideas
- A subset is a subgroup iff closed under the operation and inverses.
- Cyclic groups: $\mathbb{Z}$ (infinite) or $\mathbb{Z}_n$ (finite).
- Subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}_n$ correspond to divisors of $n$.
- Generators: the smallest subset that produces the whole group.
- Lagrange's theorem: $|H|$ divides $|G|$.

## Worked Examples
**Example 1 — Subgroup test.** $H = \{a + b \sqrt{2} : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ in $\mathbb{R}$ under addition. Closed: $(a + b \sqrt{2}) + (c + d \sqrt{2}) = (a + c) + (b + d) \sqrt{2} \in H$. Inverses: $-(a + b \sqrt{2}) = -a - b \sqrt{2} \in H$. So $H$ is a subgroup.

**Example 2 — Subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$.** Divisors: $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$. Subgroups of orders $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$, generated by $12, 6, 4, 3, 2, 1$ respectively. So subgroups are $\{0\}$, $\{0, 6\}$, $\{0, 4, 8\}$, $\{0, 3, 6, 9\}$, $\{0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10\}$, and $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ itself.

**Example 3 — Fermat's little theorem.** $3^{10} \pmod{11}$. By Fermat, $3^{10} \equiv 1 \pmod{11}$. ✓ ($3^5 = 243 = 22 \cdot 11 + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod{11}$, so $3^{10} = (3^5)^2 \equiv 1$.)

**Example 4 — Order of a matrix.** $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ in GL$(2, \mathbb{R})$. $A^2 = -I$, $A^4 = I$. So the order of $A$ is $4$. (It generates a cyclic subgroup of order $4$.)

## Common Misconceptions
- **"Every subset is a subgroup."** No — most subsets are not closed or do not contain inverses.
- **"A subgroup must contain the identity."** By definition, yes — a subgroup is a group.
- **"Cyclic groups are all the same."** They are not — $\mathbb{Z}_n$ and $\mathbb{Z}_m$ are isomorphic iff $n = m$. But all cyclic groups of the same order are isomorphic.
- **"Lagrange gives necessary and sufficient conditions."** Necessary but not sufficient: the order of a subgroup must divide $|G|$, but not every divisor is realised as a subgroup order (e.g. $A_5$ has order $60$ but no subgroup of order $15$).

## Connections
Lagrange's theorem is the foundation of the classification of finite groups. The structure theorem for finite abelian groups is a major result used in number theory and cryptography. Subgroups are the building blocks of quotient groups (next lesson), and the lattice of subgroups encodes the structure of the group.

## Takeaway
- Subgroup test: non-empty, closed under operation, contains inverses.
- Cyclic groups: $\mathbb{Z}$ (infinite) or $\mathbb{Z}_n$ (finite).
- Subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}_n$ correspond to divisors of $n$.
- Generators: a subset that produces the group by products and inverses.
- Lagrange: $|H|$ divides $|G|$.
