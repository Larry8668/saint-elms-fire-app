# Binary Operations and Group Axioms

## Overview
A *group* is a set with a binary operation that combines any two elements to give a third, in a way that satisfies four axioms. Groups are the simplest algebraic structures and the building blocks of more advanced algebra. They appear throughout physics: in the symmetries of space (rotations, translations, Lorentz transformations), in the conservation laws (Noether's theorem), in crystallography, and in the gauge symmetries of particle physics. This lesson introduces the axioms and checks them on familiar examples.

## Learning Path
- What you should already know: basic set theory, function composition, the integers.
- What this lesson adds: a precise definition of group, the verification of the axioms in examples, and the vocabulary of group theory.
- What it unlocks: subgroups, homomorphisms, quotient groups (next lessons), and the symmetry groups of physics.

## Core Explanation
**Binary operation.** A function $*: G \times G \to G$ that takes two elements of $G$ and returns a third element of $G$. The operation is *closed* by definition.

**Group axioms.** A group is a set $G$ with a binary operation $*$ such that:
1. *Closure*: $a * b \in G$ for all $a, b \in G$ (built into the definition).
2. *Associativity*: $(a * b) * c = a * (b * c)$ for all $a, b, c \in G$.
3. *Identity*: there exists $e \in G$ such that $a * e = e * a = a$ for all $a \in G$.
4. *Inverse*: for every $a \in G$, there exists $a^{-1} \in G$ such that $a * a^{-1} = a^{-1} * a = e$.

A group is *abelian* (or *commutative*) if $a * b = b * a$ for all $a, b \in G$.

**Examples of groups.**
- $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$: integers under addition. Identity $0$, inverse $-n$. Abelian.
- $(\mathbb{Q} \setminus \{0\}, \times)$: non-zero rationals under multiplication. Identity $1$, inverse $1/n$. Abelian.
- $(\mathbb{R}^n, +)$: vectors under addition. Identity $\vec{0}$, inverse $-\vec{v}$. Abelian.
- GL$(n, \mathbb{R})$: invertible $n \times n$ real matrices under multiplication. Identity $I$, inverse $A^{-1}$. Not abelian for $n \ge 2$.
- $S_n$: permutations of $n$ objects under composition. Identity is the identity permutation. Not abelian for $n \ge 3$.
- The cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}_n = \{0, 1, \ldots, n-1\}$ under addition mod $n$.

**Examples that are not groups.**
- $(\mathbb{N}, +)$: natural numbers, no inverse for most elements.
- $(\mathbb{Z}, \times)$: zero has no inverse.
- $\{1, 2, 3\}$ under multiplication mod something: depends on the mod.

**Order of a group.** The number of elements $|G|$. Finite groups have finite order; infinite groups have infinite order. The cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}_n$ has order $n$.

**Uniqueness of identity and inverse.** The identity is unique (proof: if $e, e'$ are both identities, then $e = e * e' = e'$). The inverse of $a$ is unique: if $a * b = e$ and $a * c = e$, then $b = b * e = b * (a * c) = (b * a) * c = e * c = c$. These proofs use only the axioms.

**Cancellation.** If $a * b = a * c$, then $b = c$ (left cancellation). If $b * a = c * a$, then $b = c$ (right cancellation). Proof: multiply by $a^{-1}$ on the left (or right).

**Powers and multiples.** In a group written multiplicatively, $a^n$ is $a * a * \cdots * a$ ($n$ times) for $n > 0$; $a^0 = e$; $a^{-n} = (a^{-1})^n$. In an additive group, $na = a + a + \cdots + a$ ($n$ times); $0 a = 0$; $(-n) a = -(na)$.

**Cyclic subgroups.** For any $a \in G$, the set $\{a^n : n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is a subgroup, called the cyclic subgroup generated by $a$. The order of $a$ is the smallest positive $n$ (if it exists) with $a^n = e$; otherwise $a$ has infinite order.

**Direct products.** Given groups $G$ and $H$, the direct product $G \times H$ is the set of pairs $(g, h)$ with componentwise operation: $(g_1, h_1) * (g_2, h_2) = (g_1 g_2, h_1 h_2)$. Identity: $(e_G, e_H)$. Inverse: $(g, h)^{-1} = (g^{-1}, h^{-1})$.

**Matrix groups.** GL$(n, \mathbb{R})$ is the set of $n \times n$ invertible real matrices. The *special linear group* SL$(n, \mathbb{R})$ is the subgroup with determinant $1$. The *orthogonal group* O$(n)$ is the subgroup with $A^T A = I$. The *special orthogonal group* SO$(n) = $ O$(n) \cap$ SL$(n, \mathbb{R})$ is the group of rotations in $n$ dimensions. The *unitary group* U$(n)$ is the complex analog.

**Group tables.** For a finite group, the Cayley table (multiplication table) gives the operation. Each row and column is a permutation of the elements (a consequence of the existence of inverses).

**Subgroups.** A subset $H \subseteq G$ is a *subgroup* if it is closed under the operation, contains the identity, and contains inverses. (Equivalently, $H$ is a group under the same operation.) Trivial subgroups: $\{e\}$ and $G$ itself.

**Lagrange's theorem (preview).** The order of a subgroup divides the order of the group. This is the first major theorem of group theory and is proved in the next module.

**The integers mod $n$.** $\mathbb{Z}_n$ with addition mod $n$ is a cyclic group of order $n$. With multiplication mod $n$, $(\mathbb{Z}_n, \times)$ is not a group (zero divisors have no inverse), but $(\mathbb{Z}_n^*, \times)$ (the units) is.

**Roots of unity.** The $n$-th roots of unity in $\mathbb{C}$ form a cyclic group of order $n$ under multiplication: $\{e^{2 \pi i k/n} : k = 0, 1, \ldots, n-1\}$.

**Symmetry groups in physics.** The group of spatial translations $(\mathbb{R}^3, +)$. The rotation group SO$(3)$ (in 3D). The Lorentz group (in special relativity). The gauge group U$(1) \times$ SU$(2) \times$ SU$(3)$ of the Standard Model of particle physics. The classification of particles and interactions rests on these symmetry groups.

**Noether's theorem (preview).** Every continuous symmetry of a physical system corresponds to a conservation law. Translation symmetry → linear momentum conservation. Rotation symmetry → angular momentum conservation. Time translation → energy conservation. U$(1)$ gauge symmetry → electric charge conservation.

## Key Ideas
- A group is a set with an associative operation, identity, and inverses.
- Closure is built into the binary operation.
- Abelian if commutative; many groups are not.
- Identity and inverses are unique.
- Subgroups are subsets that are themselves groups.

## Worked Examples
**Example 1 — Integers mod 5.** $\mathbb{Z}_5 = \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4\}$ under addition mod 5. Identity $0$. Inverses: $0^{-1} = 0, 1^{-1} = 4, 2^{-1} = 3, 3^{-1} = 2, 4^{-1} = 1$. Cyclic of order $5$.

**Example 2 — $S_3$.** Permutations of $\{1, 2, 3\}$. Six elements: identity, three transpositions, two 3-cycles. Not abelian (e.g. $(1 2)(1 3) = (1 3 2) \ne (1 3)(1 2) = (1 2 3)$).

**Example 3 — Cyclic subgroup.** In GL$(2, \mathbb{R})$, take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. $A^2 = -I$, $A^3 = -A$, $A^4 = I$. So $\langle A \rangle = \{I, A, -I, -A\}$, a cyclic subgroup of order $4$.

**Example 4 — Direct product.** $\mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_3$ is the cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}_6$ (because $\gcd(2, 3) = 1$). In general, $\mathbb{Z}_m \times \mathbb{Z}_n \cong \mathbb{Z}_{mn}$ iff $\gcd(m, n) = 1$.

## Common Misconceptions
- **"All groups are abelian."** No — most groups are not. Abelian is a special property.
- **"A subgroup must contain the identity."** A subgroup is required to be a group under the same operation, so yes.
- **"Closure is an extra axiom."** It is built into the definition of a binary operation on $G$.
- **"Every group is cyclic."** Only some are. $\mathbb{Z}_6$ is cyclic; $S_3$ is not.

## Connections
Group theory is the language of symmetry in physics. The classification of elementary particles uses the representation theory of the Lorentz group and the Standard Model gauge group. The discrete groups (especially $S_n$, the dihedral groups, the point groups) classify crystal symmetries. The continuous groups (Lie groups) — SO$(3)$, SU$(2)$, U$(1)$, etc. — are the structure groups of the fundamental forces.

- Subgroups are subsets that are groups.
