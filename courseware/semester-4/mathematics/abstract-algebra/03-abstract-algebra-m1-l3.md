# Permutation Groups and Cayley Tables

## Overview
The symmetric group $S_n$ is the canonical example of a finite non-abelian group, and its elements — permutations — are the language of combinatorics. This lesson develops the tools for working with permutations (cycle notation, parity, conjugacy classes) and the basic tool for any finite group — the Cayley table. We also discuss generating sets, the centre, and how the group is built from its subgroups.

## Learning Path
- What you should already know: the group axioms, basic permutation notation, the symmetric group $S_n$.
- What this lesson adds: cycle composition, order of an element, Cayley tables, the centre, and conjugacy classes.
- What it unlocks: subgroups, quotient groups, homomorphisms (next module), and the classification of finite groups.

## Core Explanation
**Cayley table.** For a finite group $G$, the Cayley table is a $|G| \times |G|$ array whose $(g, h)$ entry is $g * h$. Each row and column contains each element exactly once (a consequence of the existence of inverses). The table determines the group up to isomorphism.

**Cycle notation.** A permutation is written as a product of disjoint cycles. The cycle $(a_1 a_2 \ldots a_k)$ sends $a_1 \to a_2, a_2 \to a_3, \ldots, a_k \to a_1$. The order of a cycle is its length. Disjoint cycles commute.

**Example.** $(1 2 3)(4 5)$ in $S_5$ has order $\text{lcm}(3, 2) = 6$.

**Composition.** Permutations are composed right-to-left: $\sigma \tau$ means "first $\tau$, then $\sigma$". For example, $\sigma = (1 2 3)$ and $\tau = (2 3)$: $\sigma \tau$ sends $1 \to 1$ (fixed), $2 \to 3$ (by $\tau$, then by $\sigma$? no: $2 \to 3$ by $\tau$, then $3 \to 1$ by $\sigma$, so $2 \to 1$); $3 \to 2$. So $\sigma \tau = (1 3 2)$.

**Transposition.** A 2-cycle, e.g. $(i j)$. Every permutation is a product of transpositions. The *sign* of a permutation is $(-1)^k$ where $k$ is the number of transpositions in any decomposition (the sign is well-defined).

**Even and odd permutations.** Even permutations form a subgroup $A_n \subset S_n$ of order $n!/2$, the *alternating group*. $A_n$ is normal in $S_n$. $A_5$ is the smallest non-abelian simple group.

**Generators.** A subset $S \subseteq G$ *generates* $G$ if every element of $G$ is a product of elements of $S$ and their inverses. $S_n$ is generated by transpositions, or by the two permutations $(1 2)$ and $(1 2 3 \ldots n)$. $\mathbb{Z}_n$ is generated by $1$.

**Order of an element.** The smallest positive $n$ (if it exists) such that $g^n = e$. For $\mathbb{Z}_n$, the element $k$ has order $n / \gcd(n, k)$. For permutations, the order is the lcm of the cycle lengths.

**Centre of a group.** $Z(G) = \{g \in G : g h = h g \text{ for all } h \in G\}$. The centre is always a normal subgroup. $Z(\mathbb{Z}_n) = \mathbb{Z}_n$ (abelian). $Z(S_n) = \{e\}$ for $n \ge 3$. $Z(\text{GL}(n)) = \{c I : c \in \mathbb{R}^*\}$ (scalar matrices).

**Conjugacy.** $g$ and $h$ are *conjugate* if $h = x g x^{-1}$ for some $x \in G$. Conjugacy is an equivalence relation. The equivalence classes are the *conjugacy classes*. In $S_n$, two permutations are conjugate iff they have the same cycle type (same number of cycles of each length).

**Conjugacy class size.** $|C(g)| = |G|/|N_G(g)|$, where $N_G(g) = \{x \in G : x g x^{-1} = g\}$ is the *normaliser* of $g$. The number of conjugacy classes is the number of partitions of $n$ (for $S_n$).

**Group actions.** A *group action* of $G$ on a set $X$ is a map $G \times X \to X$ satisfying $e \cdot x = x$ and $(g h) \cdot x = g \cdot (h \cdot x)$. Every permutation group acts on a set; the *orbit* of $x$ is $\{g \cdot x : g \in G\}$; the *stabiliser* is $\{g : g \cdot x = x\}$.

**Orbit–stabiliser theorem.** $|G| = |\text{orbit}(x)| \times |\text{stabiliser}(x)|$. This is one of the most useful theorems of group theory and is a consequence of the bijection between the orbit and the cosets of the stabiliser.

**Lagrange's theorem.** The order of a subgroup divides the order of the group. The order of any element divides the order of the group. The proof uses the equivalence relation on $G$ defined by $g_1 \sim g_2$ iff $g_1 g_2^{-1} \in H$, with equivalence classes = cosets of $H$.

**Cosets.** $g H = \{g h : h \in H\}$ is a *left coset* of $H$ in $G$. Cosets partition $G$, and the number of cosets is the *index* $[G : H] = |G|/|H|$ (which is an integer by Lagrange's theorem).

**Normal subgroups.** $H \trianglelefteq G$ iff $g H g^{-1} = H$ for all $g \in G$ (equivalently, $gH = Hg$ for all $g$). Normal subgroups are the kernels of homomorphisms and the building blocks of quotient groups.

**Quotient groups (preview).** If $H$ is normal in $G$, the set of cosets $G/H$ is a group under $(gH)(g'H') = (g g') H$. $|G/H| = |G|/|H|$. Examples: $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} = \mathbb{Z}_n$; $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$ is a circle (uncountable).

**Examples of Cayley tables.** $\mathbb{Z}_3 = \{0, 1, 2\}$ with $+$ mod $3$:
$$\begin{array}{c|ccc} + & 0 & 1 & 2 \\ \hline 0 & 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 2 & 2 & 0 & 1 \end{array}$$

$V_4 = \mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2 = \{e, a, b, ab\}$ with $a^2 = b^2 = e$, $ab = ba$:
$$\begin{array}{c|cccc} * & e & a & b & ab \\ \hline e & e & a & b & ab \\ a & a & e & ab & b \\ b & b & ab & e & a \\ ab & ab & b & a & e \end{array}$$

**Recognition of small groups.** Up to isomorphism:
- Order $1$: trivial group.
- Order $2$: $\mathbb{Z}_2$.
- Order $3$: $\mathbb{Z}_3$.
- Order $4$: $\mathbb{Z}_4$ or $V_4$ (Klein four).
- Order $5$: $\mathbb{Z}_5$.
- Order $6$: $\mathbb{Z}_6$ or $S_3$.
- Order $7$: $\mathbb{Z}_7$.
- Order $8$: $\mathbb{Z}_8$, $\mathbb{Z}_4 \times \mathbb{Z}_2$, $\mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2$, $D_8$, $Q_8$.

## Key Ideas
- A Cayley table determines a finite group.
- The order of an element is the lcm of cycle lengths (in $S_n$).
- Conjugacy classes partition $G$; in $S_n$, they correspond to cycle types.
- Lagrange's theorem: $|H|$ divides $|G|$ for any subgroup $H$.
- Normal subgroups allow quotient groups $G/H$.

## Worked Examples
**Example 1 — Permutation composition.** $\sigma = (1 2 3), \tau = (1 2)$. Compute $\sigma \tau$. $\tau$ acts first: $1 \to 2, 2 \to 1, 3 \to 3$. Then $\sigma$: $2 \to 3, 1 \to 2, 3 \to 1$. Combined: $1 \to 2 \to 3$, so $1 \to 3$. $2 \to 1 \to 2$, so $2 \to 2$. $3 \to 3 \to 1$, so $3 \to 1$. So $\sigma \tau = (1 3)$.

**Example 2 — Sign of a permutation.** $\sigma = (1 2)(3 4 5)$. Two cycles of lengths $2$ and $3$. Sign: $(-1)^{(2 - 1) + (3 - 1)} = (-1)^{3} = -1$. So $\sigma$ is odd.

**Example 3 — Centre of $D_8$.** $D_8 = \{e, r, r^2, r^3, s, sr, sr^2, sr^3\}$. The centre is $\{e, r^2\}$ — the only element that commutes with all the others.

**Example 4 — Lagrange.** $A_4$ (even permutations of 4 elements) has order $4!/2 = 12$. Its subgroups include $V_4 = \{e, (1 2)(3 4), (1 3)(2 4), (1 4)(2 3)\}$ of order $4$ — divides $12$ ✓.

## Common Misconceptions
- **"Order of an element = order of the group."** The order of an element divides the order of the group (Lagrange), but they are not equal in general.
- **"Every subgroup is normal."** Only in abelian groups. In $S_3$, the subgroup $\{e, (1 2)\}$ is not normal.
- **"Conjugate elements have the same order."** True. Conjugate elements have the same algebraic properties.
- **"The Cayley table is unique."** It is, up to relabelling of the elements. Two Cayley tables that look different can be isomorphic groups.

## Connections
Group actions, conjugacy classes, and the orbit–stabiliser theorem are the tools for counting, for the classification of finite groups, and for the representation theory used in particle physics. The symmetric group $S_n$ is the universal finite group (Cayley's theorem). Quotient groups are the foundation of Galois theory and algebraic topology.

## Takeaway
- The Cayley table determines a finite group.
- The order of a permutation is the lcm of cycle lengths.
- Conjugacy classes partition the group; same cycle type in $S_n$.
- Lagrange: $|H|$ divides $|G|$.
- Normal subgroups give quotient groups.
