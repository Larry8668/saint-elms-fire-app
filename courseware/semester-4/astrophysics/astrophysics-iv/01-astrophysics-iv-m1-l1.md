# The Structure of the Milky Way

## Overview

We study the Milky Way from inside it — like mapping a forest while standing among the trees. That handicap shaped the subject's history: for centuries the Galaxy's size, shape, and our position in it were unknown, obscured by dust and by our own location in the disc. This lesson reconstructs the Galaxy from the outside in as modern observations reveal it: a barred spiral with a thin disc of young stars and gas, a central bulge, an extended stellar halo, and a dominant invisible halo of dark matter. We will also see how radio waves cut through the dust to map spiral arms, how the rotation curve exposes mass we cannot see, and how stars whipping around Sagittarius A* prove a four-million-solar-mass black hole sits at the centre.

## Learning Path

1. Inventory the Galaxy's components: thin disc, thick disc, bulge/bar, stellar halo, dark halo — each with its characteristic populations.
2. Understand why optical surveys mislead (extinction, Sun's disc location) and how Shapley used globular clusters to find the true centre.
3. Learn the 21-cm line of neutral hydrogen and how Doppler shifts turn it into a map of spiral arms.
4. Derive mass from circular orbits and read the flat rotation curve as evidence for dark matter.
5. Follow the stellar orbits around Sgr A* to the supermassive black hole.
6. Place the Galaxy in context: dimensions, masses, and its role as the template galaxy we know best.

## Core Explanation

### The components

The Milky Way is a barred spiral, roughly 30 kpc across the stellar disc, with these main parts:

| Component | Contents | Character |
|-----------|----------|-----------|
| Thin disc | Gas, dust, young stars (Population I), spiral arms | Scale height ~300 pc; site of ongoing star formation |
| Thick disc | Older, metal-poorer disc stars | Scale height ~1 kpc; likely from ancient mergers/heating |
| Bulge/bar | Old stars, peanut-shaped bar ~3 kpc long | Population II-rich centre, with a nuclear stellar disc |
| Stellar halo | Globular clusters, ancient metal-poor stars, streams | Spherical, sparse, ~50 kpc extent |
| Dark halo | Non-luminous mass | Dominates total mass; extends hundreds of kpc |

The two stellar populations codify this: **Population I** (young, metal-rich, disc) and **Population II** (old, metal-poor, halo/bulge). The terminology is historical — Population I and II in reverse discovery order — but the physics is real: metallicity traces how many prior generations enriched the gas before a star formed.

### Finding the centre: Shapley's globular clusters

Early 20th-century star counts placed the Sun near the Galaxy's centre — wrong, because dust in the disc hides everything beyond a few kiloparsecs in visible light. Harlow Shapley (1918) broke the impasse by mapping the distances to globular clusters, which live in the dust-free halo. Their distribution is strongly asymmetric, concentrated toward Sagittarius — the true Galactic Centre, ~8 kpc from the Sun. The Galaxy turned out to be far larger, and our position far less central, than the optical sky suggested.

Modern values: distance to the Centre R₀ ≈ 8.2 kpc; the Sun orbits at ~220–240 km/s with a period ~230 Myr (a "Galactic year").

### Mapping arms with the 21-cm line

Visible light fails through the disc, but the hyperfine spin-flip transition of neutral hydrogen emits at 1420 MHz (λ = 21.1 cm), penetrating dust freely. Observing the 21-cm line in different directions gives:

- **Column density** from line intensity — where the gas is.
- **Radial velocity** from the Doppler shift — how the gas moves along the line of sight.

Inside the Sun's orbit, circular-rotation geometry converts radial velocity into distance (kinematic distance), assembling maps that revealed the spiral arms — the Perseus, Sagittarius, Scutum-Centaurus arms and local features like the Orion Spur. Modern radio surveys (HI4PI, THOR) plus Gaia parallaxes refine the picture into a four-armed, barred structure.

### The rotation curve and dark matter

For any mass distribution, a star on a circular orbit at radius r obeys:

v²(r) = G M(< r) / r → M(< r) = v² r / G

If the Galaxy's mass were concentrated like its light (stars and gas in the inner ~15 kpc), velocities should fall Keplerian outside: v ∝ r^−1/2. The observed rotation curve is instead **flat**: v stays near 220–240 km/s from the inner kiloparsecs out to the measurement limit and beyond.

Flat means M(< r) ∝ r — mass keeps growing linearly where almost no light remains. The luminous Galaxy is embedded in a massive, extended **dark matter halo** containing most of the Galaxy's mass: baryonic mass ~6 × 10¹⁰ M_☉ versus total (dynamical) mass ~10¹² M_☉. The rotation curve is the classic first evidence for dark matter, encountered again at cosmological scales in Module 2.

### Sagittarius A*: the central black hole

The radio source Sagittarius A* marks the dynamical centre. Since the 1990s, infrared adaptive-optics campaigns (Keck, VLT) have tracked individual stars in tight orbits around it. The star S2 (period ~16 yr) passes within ~120 AU at ~7650 km/s; Kepler's third law applied to such orbits gives the enclosed mass:

M ≈ 4.0 × 10⁶ M_☉ within ~120 AU

No stable cluster of stars, neutron stars, or stellar black holes can pack that mass so tightly — general relativity's only viable explanation is a supermassive black hole. The Event Horizon Telescope's 2022 image of Sgr A*'s shadow confirmed it directly. This 4-million-solar-mass anchor, and the scaling of black-hole mass with host properties, sets the stage for Module 1's galaxy lessons and the AGN physics of lesson 3.

## Key Ideas

- The Milky Way is a barred spiral: thin/thick discs, bulge/bar, stellar halo, and a dominant dark matter halo.
- Populations I and II encode chemical history: young metal-rich disc stars versus old metal-poor halo stars.
- Dust hides the distant disc in visible light; Shapley circumvented it using halo globular clusters to locate the Centre ~8 kpc away.
- The 21-cm line maps neutral hydrogen and, via Doppler shifts, the spiral arms.
- The flat rotation curve implies M(< r) ∝ r far beyond the visible disc — the dark halo holds most of the mass.
- Stellar orbits around Sgr A* prove a ~4 × 10⁶ M_☉ supermassive black hole.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1 — Galactic year.**
Sun's orbital speed v ≈ 230 km/s at R₀ = 8.2 kpc = 2.5 × 10²⁰ m:

P = 2πR/v = 1.59 × 10²¹ / 2.3 × 10⁵ ≈ 6.9 × 10¹⁵ s ≈ 220 Myr

The Sun has completed ~20 orbits since its birth.

**Example 2 — Mass from the rotation curve.**
At r = 8.2 kpc with v = 230 km/s:

M(< r) = v²r/G = (2.3 × 10⁵)² × 2.5 × 10²⁰ / 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ ≈ 2.0 × 10⁴¹ kg ≈ 10¹¹ M_☉

The interior-to-Sun mass; extending to 50 kpc with the flat curve multiplies this several-fold — far exceeding the stellar and gas inventory.

**Example 3 — 21-cm kinematics.**
An HI cloud on the tangent point toward Galactic longitude l = 30° has maximum radial velocity v_r = v_max = v₀(1 − sin l) ≈ 230 × (1 − 0.5) = 115 km/s for a flat curve with v₀ = 230 km/s. Such tangent-point velocities anchor rotation-curve measurements without needing independent distances.

**Example 4 — Sgr A* mass from S2.**
S2: period P ≈ 16 yr, semi-major axis a ≈ 1000 AU ≈ 1.5 × 10¹⁴ m. Kepler's third law:

M = 4π²a³/(GP²) = 4π²(3.4 × 10⁴²)/(6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 2.5 × 10¹⁷) ≈ 8 × 10³⁶ kg ≈ 4 × 10⁶ M_☉

Four million solar masses — and the pericentre passage tightens the enclosed volume still further.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"We can photograph the Milky Way from outside."** Every external galaxy image is of a *different* galaxy; the Milky Way's face-on appearance is inferred from structure, not photographed.
2. **"The Galaxy's centre is where the most stars appear."** The crowded Sagittarius star clouds hint at it, but dust extinction makes the true centre invisible optically; radio and infrared reveal it.
3. **"Flat rotation curves are a measurement error."** They are reproduced in HI, CO, and stellar kinematics across hundreds of galaxies — the cornerstone evidence for dark matter.
4. **"The Sun sits at the Galaxy's edge."** We are mid-disc, ~8 kpc from the centre in a ~15 kpc-radius stellar disc — a suburban location.
5. **"Sgr A* is active like a quasar."** It is accreting at a trickle (~10⁻⁹ Eddington) — luminous only in X-ray flares; it is a dormant supermassive black hole.

## Quick Check

1. Name the Milky Way's five main components and one distinguishing property of each.
2. Why did early star counts misplace the Sun at the Galaxy's centre, and how did Shapley correct this?
3. What does a flat rotation curve imply about how mass is distributed in the Galaxy?
4. How does the 21-cm line allow mapping of spiral arms despite dust?
5. What observation proves Sgr A* is a supermassive black hole rather than a dense star cluster?

**Answers:**
1. Thin disc (young stars/gas, ongoing star formation); thick disc (older heated population); bulge/bar (old central stars, ~3 kpc bar); stellar halo (globular clusters, metal-poor); dark halo (dominant mass, invisible).
2. Dust extinction limited optical sightlines to a few kpc, making the nearby disc look symmetric. Shapley mapped dust-free halo globular clusters, whose centroid lies toward Sagittarius ~8 kpc away.
3. M(< r) grows roughly linearly with r where little light remains — most mass is in an extended dark halo, not the luminous components.
4. The 21.1 cm hyperfine emission penetrates dust; intensity gives gas location and Doppler shift gives velocity, which rotation geometry converts to distance inside the solar circle.
5. Stars in tight Keplerian orbits (e.g. S2) enclose ~4 × 10⁶ M_☉ within ~120 AU — too dense for any stable stellar configuration; plus the EHT shadow image.

## Takeaway

The Galaxy is knowable from within: clusters betray the centre, radio waves trace the arms, orbits weigh the centre, and the rotation curve weighs everything — revealing that most of it is invisible. The Milky Way is our calibration object, the one galaxy we know deeply enough to use as the template for all the rest.
