# Library, E-Resources and Reading Lists

## Overview

The university library is one of your most valuable resources. Beyond the physical books and journals, the library provides access to electronic databases, e-books, and e-journals that would be impossible for an individual student to afford. This lesson explains how to navigate the physical library, access the electronic resources, and use reading lists strategically. Whether you are looking for a classic textbook, a recent research paper, or a popular science article, the library is the place to start. The lesson is essential for new students, but the skills it teaches are valuable throughout your academic and professional life.

## Learning Path

- **What you should already know**: how to read your course syllabus; the names of the courses you are taking (Lesson m1-l1).
- **What this lesson adds**: confident use of the library and its electronic resources; a strategy for working through a reading list.
- **What later lessons this will unlock**: code of conduct and grievance redressal (Lesson m2-l3); research projects in later years of the programme.

## Core Explanation

### The physical library

A university library typically contains:

- **Stacks**: the main collection of books, organised by subject. Most Indian universities use the Dewey Decimal Classification (DDC) or the Library of Congress Classification. Each book has a call number that places it in a specific location on the shelves.
- **Reference section**: encyclopaedias, dictionaries, handbooks, and other reference works that cannot be borrowed. These are for in-library use only.
- **Periodicals section**: current and back issues of journals and magazines, often in bound volumes. Many journals have moved online, but the print archive is often still available.
- **Reading rooms**: quiet spaces for individual study. Some libraries have group study rooms for collaborative work.
- **Special collections**: rare books, archives, theses, and other materials of historical interest. Access may require permission from the librarian.
- **Digital zone**: computers, printers, scanners, and Wi-Fi access.

When you enter the library, the first stop is the **catalogue** — a searchable database of the library's holdings. The catalogue is usually accessible through the library website and through dedicated terminals in the library. You can search by title, author, subject, keyword, or call number. The catalogue record gives the call number, the location (which floor or wing), and the availability (on the shelf, checked out, in reserve).

To find a book on the shelves:

1. Search the catalogue for the title or author.
2. Note the call number and the location.
3. Go to the right floor and wing.
4. Read the shelf labels; books are arranged in call-number order, so the book you want will be near the books with similar call numbers.
5. If the book is not on the shelf, check the catalogue again — it may be checked out, in reserve, or missing.

### Borrowing rules

Most libraries allow students to borrow a certain number of books for a certain period:

- **Loan period**: typically 2 weeks for regular books, 1 week for high-demand books, 1 day for reference and reserve books.
- **Renewal**: most books can be renewed once or twice, either online or at the circulation desk, unless another student has placed a hold.
- **Late fees**: most libraries charge a small late fee per day per book. The fee accumulates until the book is returned; very late returns can result in a suspension of borrowing privileges.
- **Lost books**: if you lose a book, you must pay the replacement cost plus a processing fee. The fee is typically 1.5 to 2 times the list price.
- **Reserves**: high-demand books (e.g. textbooks) are placed on reserve and can be borrowed for a few hours at a time.

To avoid problems, return or renew books before the due date, and never lend your library card to another student.

### Electronic resources

Most university libraries subscribe to a range of electronic resources:

- **E-books**: electronic versions of textbooks and monographs. Common platforms: Springer Link, Wiley Online Library, EBSCOhost, ProQuest, ScienceDirect.
- **E-journals**: electronic versions of academic journals, including back issues. Common platforms: JSTOR, IEEE Xplore, American Physical Society, American Institute of Physics.
- **Databases**: searchable indexes of journal articles, conference proceedings, and other publications. Common databases: Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar (which is free but not a database in the same sense).
- **Citation databases**: tools for finding articles that cite a given paper, for tracking citations to your own work, and for calculating the h-index of an author. Web of Science and Scopus are the most common.
- **Reference management software**: tools for organising references, formatting bibliographies, and inserting citations into documents. EndNote, Zotero, and Mendeley are common.
- **Plagiarism detection**: tools for checking the originality of student work. Turnitin and Urkund are the most common. Your instructor may submit your assignment to one of these tools.

To access e-resources:

1. Go to the library website.
2. Click on the e-resources link.
3. Log in with your student credentials (student ID and password).
4. Search for the resource you need.
5. Download the PDF or read it online.

If you are off-campus, you may need to use a VPN or the library's proxy server. The library website has instructions for off-campus access.

### Reading lists

A **reading list** is a list of resources recommended or required for a course. It usually includes:

- **Textbooks**: the main book for the course, often required.
- **Reference books**: additional books that cover specific topics in more depth.
- **Journal articles**: specific research papers, often classics or recent breakthroughs.
- **Review articles**: longer articles that survey a field, useful for getting an overview.
- **Web resources**: specific websites, videos, or online simulations that supplement the material.

A typical reading list has 1 required textbook and 5–10 supplementary resources per module. The instructor may assign specific resources to read; alternatively, the reading list may be a menu from which you choose.

A good strategy for working through a reading list:

1. **Start with the required textbook**: read the chapters corresponding to each lecture, ideally before the lecture. This is the single most effective study habit.
2. **Read the assigned articles**: when the instructor assigns a specific article, read it carefully, take notes, and bring questions to the class.
3. **Skim the reference books**: you don't need to read every page of every book. Use the index and the table of contents to find the relevant sections.
4. **Use review articles to fill gaps**: if a topic in class is unclear, search for a review article on the topic. Review articles are more accessible than primary research papers.
5. **Use reference management software**: import each reference as you find it, with a brief note about why it is relevant. By the end of the course, you will have a well-organised bibliography.
6. **Don't read passively**: take notes, ask questions, and discuss the material with classmates. The goal of reading is to understand, not to finish.

### Evaluating sources

Not all sources are equally reliable. A hierarchy of reliability:

- **Peer-reviewed journal articles**: the most reliable. They have been reviewed by experts in the field before publication.
- **Conference proceedings**: papers presented at conferences. Usually peer-reviewed, but the review may be less rigorous than for a journal.
- **Textbooks**: usually reliable, but may be out of date. Check the publication date and the list of references.
- **Theses**: original research; the quality varies. The introduction of a thesis is often a good review of the field.
- **Preprints**: papers posted on arXiv or similar servers before peer review. The science is usually sound, but the paper has not been formally vetted. Use with caution.
- **Encyclopaedias**: Britannica, Wikipedia, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. Good for getting an overview, but not for citing in a research paper.
- **Popular science articles**: useful for context and motivation, but not authoritative. Examples: Scientific American, New Scientist, Quanta Magazine.
- **Websites**: anything can be posted online. Be especially wary of sites with no author, no date, and no references. Educational institutions (.edu) and government agencies (.gov) are generally more reliable than commercial sites.

When in doubt, ask your instructor or the librarian for help in evaluating a source.

### Time management for reading

Reading a textbook or a paper takes time. A rough rule of thumb: a difficult textbook takes about 10 pages per hour; a journal article takes 1–2 hours including taking notes. For a 3-credit course with 9 modules, the required reading is typically 500–1000 pages or 30–50 articles, or 50–100 hours over the semester.

Plan your reading:

- **Schedule the reading**: assign specific reading to specific days and times. Treat the reading session as a class.
- **Read in a distraction-free environment**: a library reading room is ideal.
- **Use the Pomodoro technique**: 25 minutes of focused reading, 5-minute break, repeat. After 4 cycles, take a longer break.
- **Take notes while reading**: write a 1-2 sentence summary of each section in your own words. This is more effective than highlighting.
- **Discuss the reading**: form a study group with classmates and discuss the assigned reading. The discussion will reveal gaps in your understanding.

## Key Ideas

- The physical library contains stacks, reference, periodicals, reading rooms, and special collections; use the catalogue to find books.
- Borrowing rules include loan periods, renewals, late fees, and lost-book policies.
- E-resources include e-books, e-journals, databases, citation tools, reference management software, and plagiarism detection.
- Reading lists include textbooks, reference books, journal articles, review articles, and web resources.
- Sources vary in reliability; peer-reviewed journal articles are the most reliable; websites with no author or date are the least reliable.
- Time management for reading is critical: schedule the reading, take notes, and discuss with classmates.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Finding a book in the library

You want to find the book "Introduction to Electrodynamics" by David Griffiths. Describe the steps.

**Solution.** Go to the library website and search the catalogue by title or author. The record will give the call number (e.g. QC680.G75), the location (e.g. "Main Stacks, 3rd floor"), and the availability. Go to the main stacks, find the right floor, and locate the book by its call number. The book is arranged in DDC order, so it will be near other physics textbooks with similar call numbers (QC680 is the DDC range for electricity and magnetism). If the book is checked out, you can place a hold through the catalogue.

### Example 2 — Searching a database

You want to find recent papers on "machine learning for astronomy". Describe a search strategy.

**Solution.** Go to the library website and select a database such as Scopus or Web of Science. Search for "machine learning" AND "astronomy" in the topic field. Limit the search to recent years (e.g. 2018–2024) to focus on current work. Sort by relevance or citation count. Read the abstracts to identify the most relevant papers. Download the PDFs through the library's link resolver, which will route you to the publisher's site. If the library does not subscribe to the journal, you may be able to get the paper through interlibrary loan or by emailing the author.

### Example 3 — Building a reading strategy

A course on quantum mechanics has a reading list with one required textbook (300 pages) and 5 supplementary articles (10 pages each). You have 12 weeks. Design a reading schedule.

**Solution.** Divide the textbook into 12 weekly chunks of about 25 pages each, corresponding to the lectures. Read the relevant chunk before each lecture. Allocate one week per supplementary article; read it in the week following the lecture that covered the topic. Total reading: 50 pages per week, about 5 hours per week. This is a sustainable pace and aligns the reading with the lectures, making the material more accessible.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"The library is just a place with books."** No. The library is a service organisation with expert staff, electronic resources, study spaces, and workshops. Use the librarians — they are there to help.
- **"E-resources are free on the internet."** Most academic e-resources are not free; the university pays for subscriptions. Access is through the library website with your student credentials.
- **"Reading the textbook is enough."** For most courses, the textbook is the foundation, but the journal articles, review papers, and reference books are what give you depth. A well-rounded reading strategy includes all of these.
- **"Wikipedia is not a reliable source."** Wikipedia is a useful starting point, especially for unfamiliar topics, but it should not be cited in academic work. The references at the bottom of a Wikipedia article often lead to better sources.
- **"You have to read everything on the reading list."** No. The reading list is a menu. Read the required items and as many of the supplementary items as you can. Quality of engagement matters more than quantity.

## Connections

- The library is the gateway to the academic literature; the skills you develop here are the foundation of any research career.
- Reading lists are designed by instructors to support the course material; using them strategically is one of the most effective study habits.
- E-resources and reference management tools are professional skills; familiarity with Scopus, Web of Science, EndNote, and Zotero is expected in any research or industry role.
- The habit of evaluating sources critically is essential in the age of information overload; the same skills apply to news, social media, and professional communication.
- The library is also a social space; the conversations you have with classmates in the reading rooms and group study areas are part of the learning experience.
