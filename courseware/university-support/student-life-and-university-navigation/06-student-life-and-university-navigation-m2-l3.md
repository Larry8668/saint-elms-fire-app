# Peer Contact, Privacy and the Code of Conduct

## Overview

University is a community. The relationships you build with your peers, the way you handle personal information, and your conduct inside and outside the classroom shape the experience for everyone. This lesson covers the norms of respectful peer contact, the protection of personal privacy, and the university's code of conduct. It is for new students, but the principles apply every year and in every professional context after graduation. The lesson is a complement to the academic-integrity content in Lesson m2-l1 and the office-hours and helpdesk content in Lesson m1-l2.

## Learning Path

- **What you should already know**: how to use office hours and the helpdesk (Lesson m1-l2); the rules of classroom and laboratory etiquette (Lesson m2-l1).
- **What this lesson adds**: confident, respectful interaction with peers; protection of personal privacy; understanding of the code of conduct and grievance redressal.
- **What this lesson unlocks**: a positive and productive university experience; the foundation of a professional network; readiness for the workplace.

## Core Explanation

### Peer contact norms

A university class is a small community. The relationships you build with your classmates are part of your education; many students form study groups, lifelong friendships, and professional networks. A few simple norms help these relationships thrive:

- **Use the class group respectfully**: most courses have a WhatsApp, Telegram, or other messaging group for the class. Use the group for course-related questions, sharing resources, and coordinating study sessions. Avoid spamming, off-topic messages, or messages at unreasonable hours (no messages between 10 pm and 7 am unless it is a genuine emergency).

- **Ask questions that benefit everyone**: if you have a question about a concept, post it in the class group. Someone else probably has the same question, and a class discussion often clarifies the issue. If the question is personal or sensitive, message the instructor or a specific classmate privately.

- **Be willing to help**: if a classmate asks a question you can answer, respond. Teaching is the best way to deepen your own understanding.

- **Respect different levels of engagement**: some classmates are very active in the group, others lurk. Both are valid styles.

- **Form study groups**: small groups of 3–5 students who meet regularly to work through problems, discuss readings, and prepare for exams. Study groups are one of the most effective learning strategies; they force you to articulate your understanding and to listen to other perspectives.

- **One-on-one interactions**: in study sessions, lab partners, or project teams, be reliable and respectful. Show up on time, do your share of the work, and communicate if you cannot meet a commitment.

- **No romantic or sexual harassment**: unwanted advances, comments, or contact are harassment. Universities have a zero-tolerance policy; report any incident to the grievance cell. Even seemingly mild behaviour (repeated personal questions, unwanted messages, persistent attention) can be harassment if it is unwelcome.

- **No bullying or hazing**: bullying, intimidation, and hazing have no place in a university. Report any incident.

### Personal privacy

Personal privacy is a right, and it is also a responsibility. A few guidelines:

- **Contact information**: share your phone number and email with classmates you trust. Do not share them widely or publicly. Be cautious about adding classmates to social media groups without their consent.

- **Social media**: be aware that what you post on social media is public and permanent. Universities and employers often check social media profiles; inappropriate posts can affect your academic and professional future. Set your privacy settings to limit who can see your posts, and think before you post.

- **Photographs and videos**: in the classroom, lab, and field, respect the privacy of others. Do not take photos or videos of classmates without their consent. Do not post images of classmates on social media without their consent.

- **Personal data**: do not share sensitive personal information (Aadhaar number, bank account, passwords) with classmates or over unsecured networks. Be especially careful of phishing attempts and online scams that target students.

- **Stalking and unwanted contact**: if someone is contacting you repeatedly against your wishes, document the contact (save messages, note dates and times) and report it. The university has policies against stalking and can take action.

- **Online harassment**: online harassment is as serious as offline harassment. Report any incident to the grievance cell and, if necessary, to the police.

### Code of conduct

Every university has a **code of conduct** that sets out the expected behaviour of students. The code is published in the student handbook and is binding on all students. Typical provisions include:

- **Academic integrity**: as discussed in Lesson m2-l1, your work must be your own, you must cite your sources, and you must not plagiarise, cheat, or fabricate data.

- **Respect for others**: no harassment, discrimination, bullying, or intimidation based on gender, caste, religion, region, sexual orientation, disability, or any other characteristic.

- **Respect for the institution**: no damage to university property, no theft, no unauthorised access to restricted areas.

- **Compliance with the law**: no illegal activity on campus, including drug use, violence, or possession of weapons.

- **Compliance with university rules**: attendance, dress code (where applicable), use of university facilities, parking, etc.

The code applies on campus, in university-organised activities off campus (field trips, internships, conferences), and increasingly in online spaces (social media, class groups) where your behaviour can be linked to the university.

### Grievance redressal

If you experience or witness a violation of the code of conduct, the university has a formal grievance redressal process. The typical steps:

1. **Informal resolution**: in many cases, the issue can be resolved informally. Talk to the person directly (if you feel safe), or to the instructor, the HOD, or the warden (for hostel issues). Often a conversation is enough.

2. **Formal complaint**: if informal resolution is not possible or appropriate, file a formal complaint with the grievance cell. The complaint should be in writing, factual, and include all relevant documents.

3. **Investigation**: the grievance cell investigates the complaint. This may involve interviewing the complainant, the respondent, and any witnesses. The investigation is confidential.

4. **Decision and action**: the grievance cell issues a decision and, if the complaint is upheld, recommends action. Action may include a warning, a fine, suspension, expulsion, or referral to the police.

5. **Appeal**: most universities have an appeal process. The appeal is reviewed by a higher authority.

The grievance cell is an essential part of the university's accountability structure. Use it when needed; the cell is set up to handle these situations impartially and confidentially.

### Support services

The university also offers support services for students who are going through difficult times:

- **Counselling cell**: free, confidential counselling for personal, emotional, or psychological issues. The cell has professional counsellors and student peer counsellors. Common reasons for visiting: exam stress, relationship issues, family problems, grief, anxiety, depression.

- **Mentoring programme**: many universities assign each student a faculty mentor who provides academic and personal guidance throughout the programme. The mentor can be a sounding board for any issue, academic or personal.

- **Student welfare office**: handles a range of welfare matters, including financial assistance, medical issues, and disability support.

- **International student office**: supports international students with visas, cultural adjustment, and other issues.

- **Health centre**: provides basic medical care on campus. For serious issues, refers to nearby hospitals.

- **Anti-ragging cell**: handles complaints of ragging, which is a criminal offence in India. Ragging is strictly prohibited.

- **Internal Complaints Committee (ICC)**: handles complaints of sexual harassment, as required by the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013.

### Building a positive university experience

The most important takeaways:

- **Be a positive member of the community**: be helpful, be respectful, be inclusive.
- **Build relationships**: with classmates, faculty, mentors, and support staff. These relationships are part of the value of a university education.
- **Take care of your wellbeing**: sleep, eat well, exercise, and seek help when you need it. No academic achievement is worth your health.
- **Use the support services**: the counselling cell, the mentor, the grievance cell, the ICC. They exist to help you.
- **Stay informed**: read the student handbook, the academic calendar, and the code of conduct. Know your rights and responsibilities.
- **Be the person you would want as a classmate**: kind, helpful, respectful, and engaged.

## Key Ideas

- Class groups and study groups are valuable for learning; use them respectfully.
- Personal privacy is a right and a responsibility; protect your own and respect others'.
- The code of conduct sets out the expected behaviour of students; violations have formal consequences.
- The grievance redressal process is a formal channel for serious issues; use it when informal resolution is not possible.
- Support services (counselling, mentoring, ICC, health centre) exist to help students; use them.
- The relationships and habits you build in university shape your life long after graduation.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Handling a class group issue

A classmate is posting irrelevant memes and personal complaints in the class group at all hours. It is disrupting the group's usefulness. What should you do?

**Solution.** First, ask the classmate politely, in the group, to keep the group for course-related content. If the behaviour continues, talk to the class representative or the instructor about creating ground rules for the group. Most groups develop informal norms over time; sometimes a reminder is enough.

### Example 2 — Responding to unwanted contact

A classmate is sending you personal messages repeatedly, despite your polite requests to stop. What should you do?

**Solution.** Document the messages (screenshots with timestamps). Stop responding. Tell a friend or a family member about the situation. Report the issue to the instructor, the HOD, or the grievance cell. If the behaviour continues or escalates, consider reporting to the police. You have the right to be free from unwanted contact.

### Example 3 — Filing a grievance

You have been the victim of sexual harassment by a senior student. You do not feel safe confronting them directly. What should you do?

**Solution.** Document the incident(s) with as much detail as possible: dates, times, locations, what was said or done, any witnesses. Reach out to a trusted person — a friend, a mentor, a faculty member, the counselling cell, or directly the ICC. The ICC is mandated to handle such complaints confidentially and can take action ranging from a warning to expulsion. The process is designed to protect you, not to expose you. If you feel in immediate danger, contact the campus security or the police.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"The class group is for anything and everything."** No. The class group is primarily for course-related content. Off-topic messages should be kept to a minimum, especially during high-workload periods.
- **"Privacy is a personal problem."** No. Privacy is a community issue. When you respect others' privacy, you contribute to a culture of trust. When you violate it, you harm the community.
- **"The grievance cell is only for serious cases."** No. The grievance cell handles a range of issues, from academic disputes to interpersonal conflicts. If you are unsure whether your situation warrants a complaint, you can have a confidential conversation with the cell to find out.
- **"Counselling is for weak people."** No. Counselling is for anyone who wants support. The strongest people know when to ask for help.
- **"Harassment is just flirting."** No. Flirting is mutual and welcome; harassment is unwanted and one-sided. The distinction is not about intent; it is about impact.

## Connections

- Peer relationships, privacy, and the code of conduct are part of the broader culture of the university; they shape the experience of every student.
- The grievance redressal process is a formal application of the principles of natural justice: the right to be heard, the right to a fair investigation, and the right to appeal.
- The support services (counselling, mentoring, ICC) are part of the university's investment in student welfare; they are a strength, not a weakness.
- The habits you build in university — respectful interaction, protection of privacy, use of support services — are the same habits that make for a successful professional life.
- Building a positive university experience is partly about taking advantage of the opportunities (academic, social, extracurricular) and partly about contributing to the community.
