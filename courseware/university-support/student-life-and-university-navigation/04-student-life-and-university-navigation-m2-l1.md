# Classroom, Laboratory and Field Etiquette

## Overview

Universities are professional environments, and the conventions of classroom, laboratory, and field behaviour reflect that. Punctuality, attention, and respectful interaction are basic expectations; in the laboratory, safety procedures are non-negotiable; in the field, professional conduct extends to interactions with the public, with collaborators, and with the environment. This lesson reviews the etiquette of each setting, the safety rules of the laboratory, and the conduct expected on field trips. The lesson is essential for new students and a useful refresher for everyone, because the habits you build in university will serve you in any professional context after graduation.

## Learning Path

- **What you should already know**: how to read your timetable and find your classrooms (Lesson m1-l1); how to contact faculty (Lesson m1-l2).
- **What this lesson adds**: confidence in classroom, lab, and field settings; understanding of lab safety rules and the rationale for them.

## Core Explanation

### Classroom etiquette

The classroom is a shared space; your behaviour affects the learning of everyone in the room. The basic conventions:

- **Be punctual**: arrive a few minutes before the class starts. Late entry is disruptive; the instructor may not allow entry after the class has begun.
- **Be prepared**: bring the required materials — notebook, textbook, calculator, laptop if permitted.
- **Be attentive**: silence your phone, close unrelated tabs, and avoid side conversations. The class is your primary opportunity to engage with the material.
- **Be respectful**: listen when the instructor or another student is speaking. Disagreements should be expressed constructively and at appropriate times.
- **Participate**: ask questions, answer the instructor's questions, and contribute to discussion. Passive attendance is a missed opportunity.
- **Take notes**: writing by hand (or on a tablet) is the best way to stay engaged. Research shows that students who take handwritten notes retain more than those who type.
- **No eating or drinking** (except water in a closed container): most classrooms do not allow food. Chewing gum, sipping coffee, and eating snacks are distracting.
- **No recording without permission**: video or audio recording of lectures requires the instructor's explicit permission. Photographs of the board are usually fine.
- **Leave the room clean**: take your belongings, and leave the desk and floor as you found them.

Laptops and phones are increasingly common in classrooms. The conventions vary:

- Some instructors ban laptops entirely, citing research on the negative effect of multitasking on learning.
- Some instructors allow laptops but only for note-taking; social media and email are not acceptable.
- Phones should be on silent, not vibrate (vibrate is still audible in a quiet room) and certainly not used for calls or messages.

When in doubt, ask the instructor about their specific policy. The policy may also be in the course syllabus.

### Inclusive language

The classroom is a space where everyone should feel welcome. Use **inclusive language**:

- Address the instructor by their preferred title and surname (Dr. Rao, Professor Sharma).
- Avoid assumptions about gender, religion, caste, region, or ability. Use "they" as a singular pronoun when you don't know the person's preference, and respect stated preferences.
- Avoid jokes that stereotype or demean any group. "Just kidding" is not a defence; the impact of a joke is what matters, not the intent.
- Listen actively to classmates whose perspectives differ from your own. The classroom is a place to learn from each other, not to win arguments.

If you experience or witness harassment, discrimination, or bullying, report it to the instructor, the HOD, or the grievance cell. Universities have policies against such behaviour, and reporting is the first step to addressing it.

### Laboratory safety

The laboratory is the most safety-critical part of your education. The hazards are real — chemicals, electricity, lasers, cryogens, radiation, biological materials — and the consequences of carelessness can be severe. The golden rule: **there are no small accidents in the lab**. Every incident is a chance to learn and to prevent the next one.

Before any lab session, you must:

- **Read the lab manual** for the experiment you will be doing.
- **Understand the hazards** of the equipment and chemicals you will use.
- **Wear the required PPE**: lab coat, safety goggles, gloves (where appropriate), closed-toe shoes. Long hair must be tied back; loose clothing and jewellery must be secured.
- **Know the location** of the safety equipment: fire extinguisher, eyewash station, safety shower, first-aid kit, emergency exit.

During the lab:

- **No eating, drinking, smoking, or chewing gum**.
- **No running or roughhousing**.
- **No unauthorized experiments**: do only the experiment in the manual; do not improvise.
- **Handle chemicals with care**: read the label, use the right amount, dispose of waste in the right container. Never return unused chemical to the stock bottle.
- **Handle electrical equipment with care**: keep liquids away from electrical equipment; do not touch high-voltage connections with wet hands; check cables and connectors for damage before use.
- **Handle glassware with care**: do not use cracked or chipped glassware; do not force glass joints; carry glassware in a rack or a towel.
- **Handle lasers with care**: never look directly into a laser beam, even at low power; use laser safety goggles if required; keep beams at a level below eye height.
- **Handle cryogens with care**: liquid nitrogen and helium can cause severe frostbite on contact; use cryogenic gloves and eye protection; ensure good ventilation to prevent asphyxiation.
- **Report any incident**: spills, breakages, near misses, and injuries must be reported to the lab instructor immediately, even if they seem minor.

After the lab:

- **Clean up your workspace**: wipe down the bench, return equipment and chemicals to their proper place, dispose of waste in the right container.
- **Wash your hands** thoroughly, even if you wore gloves.
- **Log out** of any computer or instrument you used.

The lab instructor is responsible for enforcing safety rules. If you are unsure about a procedure, ask before proceeding. There is no such thing as a stupid safety question.

### Field trip conduct

Field trips — to observatories, research stations, geological sites, industrial facilities, or natural areas — are an important part of a science education. They require additional care because you are in an unfamiliar environment with specific risks and norms.

Before the field trip:

- **Read the itinerary** and the safety briefing provided by the instructor.
- **Pack the right gear**: sturdy shoes, weather-appropriate clothing, water, snacks, sunscreen, hat, notebook, camera if allowed.
- **Disclose any medical conditions** (allergies, asthma, mobility issues) to the instructor so accommodations can be made.

During the field trip:

- **Stay with the group**: do not wander off on your own. The instructor counts heads at every transition.
- **Follow the instructor's instructions** promptly and without argument.
- **Respect the environment**: do not litter, do not collect samples without permission, do not disturb wildlife, do not damage plants or geological features.
- **Respect the host institution**: if you visit a research facility or industrial site, follow their specific safety and security rules. Do not touch equipment you are not authorised to use.
- **Respect the public**: if the field trip involves interaction with the public (e.g. a public outreach event), be courteous, accurate, and welcoming. You represent the university.
- **No alcohol or drugs** during the field trip, even if the day is over.
- **Document your observations** in a field notebook, including sketches, measurements, and reflections.

After the field trip:

- **Complete the assignment** (lab report, field report, or reflection) on time.
- **Thank the hosts** if the trip involved external collaborators.
- **Reflect on what you learned**: the field experience is most valuable when integrated with the classroom material.

### Academic integrity

Academic integrity is the foundation of university education. The basic rules:

- **Your work must be your own**: assignments, lab reports, and exams must reflect your own understanding and effort.
- **Collaboration is allowed** unless the instructor explicitly forbids it. When collaboration is allowed, acknowledge your collaborators clearly.
- **Cite your sources**: any idea, quote, or data that is not your own must be cited. Use the citation style required by the course (APA, MLA, Chicago, IEEE).
- **No plagiarism**: copying text from a source without citation is plagiarism, even if you paraphrase. Use quotation marks for direct quotes and cite the source.
- **No cheating**: copying from another student, using unauthorised materials in an exam, or submitting work that is not your own is cheating.
- **No fabrication**: do not make up data or sources.
- **No collusion**: do not allow another student to copy your work or submit work on their behalf.

The consequences of academic misconduct are severe: a zero on the assignment, a failing grade in the course, suspension, or expulsion. The university's policy on academic integrity is in the student handbook; read it.

## Key Ideas

- **Classroom etiquette**: punctuality, attention, respect, participation, no food or drink, no recording without permission.
- **Inclusive language**: respect for diverse backgrounds, identities, and perspectives; active listening; reporting harassment.
- **Lab safety**: PPE, no food or drink, no unauthorised experiments, careful handling of chemicals and equipment, immediate reporting of incidents.
- **Field trip conduct**: stay with the group, follow instructions, respect the environment, document observations.
- **Academic integrity**: your work is your own, cite sources, no plagiarism or cheating.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Responding to a safety concern

You notice a lab partner pouring an unknown chemical down the sink. The lab manual does not mention this chemical. What should you do?

**Solution.** Politely ask your partner to stop. Explain that disposal procedures must be followed and that the chemical must be identified before disposal. If your partner is unsure, ask the lab instructor. If the chemical has already been poured down the sink, notify the lab instructor immediately so that the appropriate spill response can be taken (which may include reporting to the safety officer). The rule is: when in doubt, do not pour it out.

### Example 2 — Handling a disruptive classmate

A classmate is talking loudly during a lecture, disturbing you and the students around you. What should you do?

**Solution.** First, ask the classmate politely to be quiet. Most people do not realise they are being disruptive. If the behaviour continues, mention it to the instructor before or after the class; the instructor can address it more directly. If the behaviour is severe or persistent, the issue can be escalated to the HOD or the grievance cell. The classroom is a shared space, and persistent disruption is a form of disrespect.

### Example 3 — Responding to a suspected case of plagiarism

You notice that a classmate's assignment has sentences that look exactly like those in a published paper. What should you do?

**Solution.** This is a difficult situation. The classmate may have plagiarised, or the similarity may be coincidental. The right thing to do is to report your concern to the instructor privately. The instructor will investigate by checking the sources and the assignment. The university has formal procedures for handling academic misconduct, and the accused student has the right to a fair hearing. As a bystander, your role is to report, not to investigate or punish.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Lab safety rules are overcautious."** No. Lab accidents are real and can be life-altering. The rules are written by people who have seen what goes wrong. Follow them.
- **"Inclusive language is just political correctness."** Inclusive language is about creating a learning environment where everyone can do their best work. It is also a professional skill that will serve you throughout your career.
- **"Field trips are a holiday."** No. Field trips are part of the curriculum; attendance is required, and the assignments are graded.
- **"Plagiarism is a minor offence."** No. Plagiarism is treated as a serious academic offence in every reputable university, with consequences ranging from a zero on the assignment to expulsion.
- **"Collaboration and cheating are the same thing."** No. Collaboration, when allowed, is working together on a problem and acknowledging the collaboration. Cheating is submitting someone else's work as your own, or using unauthorised materials in an exam.

## Connections

- Classroom, lab, and field etiquette are the foundation of professional behaviour in any workplace; the habits you build now will serve you throughout your career.
- Lab safety is a non-negotiable part of any scientific or technical profession. The principles — risk assessment, PPE, incident reporting — apply equally to a chemistry lab, a factory floor, and a hospital.
- Inclusive language and the prevention of harassment are part of the broader culture of respect that universities seek to cultivate. The classroom is a microcosm of society.
- Academic integrity is the foundation of scientific progress; without it, research is meaningless. The same rules apply to published research as to student work.
- Field trips connect classroom learning to the real world; the experience of seeing a phenomenon in nature or in a research facility is irreplaceable.
