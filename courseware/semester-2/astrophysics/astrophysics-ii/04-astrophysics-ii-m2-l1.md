# Architecture of the Solar System

## Overview
The Solar System has a clear architecture: a small number of inner rocky planets close to the Sun, a large population of asteroids between Mars and Jupiter, four outer giant planets with their own moons and ring systems, the Kuiper belt beyond Neptune, and a vast Oort cloud of comets at the edge of the Sun's gravitational influence. This lesson surveys the components and presents the modern nebular hypothesis for how the system formed.

## Learning Path
- What you should already know: Kepler's laws, basic properties of planets.
- What this lesson adds: a structural picture of the Solar System and the theory of its formation.
- What it unlocks: comparative planetology, the study of exoplanetary systems, and the connection between disk physics and planet formation.

## Core Explanation
**The Sun.** A G2V main-sequence star, mass $1.99 \times 10^{30}\text{ kg}$, radius $6.96 \times 10^8\text{ m}$, luminosity $3.83 \times 10^{26}\text{ W}$, age about $4.57$ Gyr. Contains $99.86\%$ of the Solar System's mass.

**Inner planets (terrestrial).** Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars. Small, rocky, with iron cores, no rings, few or no moons. Earth has one large moon (likely captured); Mars has two tiny captured asteroids; Mercury and Venus have none.

| Planet | $a$ (AU) | Radius ($R_\oplus$) | Mass ($M_\oplus$) | Moons |
|--------|----------|---------------------|--------------------|-------|
| Mercury | $0.39$ | $0.383$ | $0.055$ | 0 |
| Venus | $0.72$ | $0.949$ | $0.815$ | 0 |
| Earth | $1.00$ | $1.000$ | $1.000$ | 1 |
| Mars | $1.52$ | $0.532$ | $0.107$ | 2 |

**Asteroid belt.** Between Mars and Jupiter (about $2.1$–$3.3$ AU). Contains millions of small rocky bodies; total mass is only about $4\%$ of the Moon's. Ceres, the largest, is now classified as a dwarf planet. The asteroid belt is not the remains of a destroyed planet; it is a region where planet formation was prevented by Jupiter's gravitational stirring.

**Outer planets (Jovian).** Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune. Large, gaseous (hydrogen, helium) with rocky/icy cores, ring systems, and many moons.

| Planet | $a$ (AU) | Radius ($R_\oplus$) | Mass ($M_\oplus$) | Moons |
|--------|----------|---------------------|--------------------|-------|
| Jupiter | $5.20$ | $11.21$ | $317.8$ | 95+ |
| Saturn | $9.58$ | $9.45$ | $95.2$ | 146+ |
| Uranus | $19.2$ | $4.01$ | $14.5$ | 27 |
| Neptune | $30.0$ | $3.88$ | $17.1$ | 14 |

**Kuiper belt.** Beyond Neptune, from about $30$ to $50$ AU. Contains icy bodies — Pluto, Eris, Makemake, Haumea, and many smaller. Pluto is a dwarf planet. The Kuiper belt is the source of short-period comets.

**Oort cloud.** A hypothesised spherical cloud of comets at distances from about $2{,}000$ to $100{,}000$ AU. Has not been directly observed but is inferred from the existence of long-period comets with aphelia in that range. The Oort cloud is the source of long-period comets.

**Nebular hypothesis.** The Solar System formed $4.57$ Gyr ago from the gravitational collapse of a fragment of a giant molecular cloud. The initial cloud was a few light-years across and contained a few solar masses of gas and dust.

Stages of formation:
1. **Cloud collapse**: triggered by a nearby supernova or density wave, the fragment contracts under its own gravity, conserving angular momentum.
2. **Disk formation**: the rotating cloud flattens into a *protoplanetary disk* with a hot central protostar.
3. **Planetesimal formation**: dust grains stick together by electrostatic forces, growing into km-sized planetesimals.
4. **Planetary embryos**: planetesimals collide and merge, building Moon-to-Mars-sized bodies.
5. **Runaway growth**: the largest embryos dominate, sweeping up remaining material.
6. **Gas accretion (outer planets)**: once an embryo reaches $\sim 10 M_\oplus$, it accretes gas rapidly, becoming a gas giant.
7. **Late heavy bombardment**: the last major impacts, recorded in the lunar maria and other surfaces.

**Angular momentum problem.** The Sun contains $99.9\%$ of the mass but only about $0.5\%$ of the angular momentum. The rest is in the orbital motion of the planets. The mechanism that transferred angular momentum outward is magnetic braking and disk winds — the young Sun's magnetic field, coupled to the disk, acted as a brake on the Sun's rotation while spinning up the disk.

**Exoplanet diversity.** Most exoplanet systems are very different from ours: hot Jupiters, super-Earths, compact multi-planet systems. This is one of the surprises of modern astronomy. Our Solar System is not a typical outcome of planet formation.

## Key Ideas
- Inner planets: small, rocky, no rings; outer planets: large, gaseous, with rings and many moons.
- Asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter; Kuiper belt beyond Neptune; Oort cloud at the edge.
- The Solar System formed from a collapsing molecular cloud fragment, $4.57$ Gyr ago.
- Planetesimals grow into embryos; gas accretion produces gas giants.
- Most of the angular momentum is in the planets, not the Sun.

## Worked Examples
**Example 1 — Period of Jupiter.** From Kepler's third law, $T^2 = a^3$ (in years and AU). $a = 5.2$ AU: $T = \sqrt{5.2^3} = \sqrt{140.6} \approx 11.86$ years. ✓

**Example 2 — Total mass of the asteroid belt.** About $4 \times 10^{21}\text{ kg}$, or about $4\%$ of the Moon's mass. Spread over millions of bodies, the average separation is millions of km — that's why spacecraft can fly through without hitting anything.

**Example 3 — Escape velocity from the Solar System at Earth's orbit.** $v_\text{esc} = \sqrt{2 G M_\odot / a} = \sqrt{2 \times 1.33 \times 10^{20} / 1.5 \times 10^{11}} \approx 42\text{ km/s}$. Earth's orbital speed is about $30\text{ km/s}$, so a spacecraft needs an extra $\sim 12\text{ km/s}$ to escape.

## Common Misconceptions
- **"The asteroid belt is dense."** It is not — typical separations are millions of km, and the total mass is tiny.
- **"Pluto is a planet."** No — the IAU reclassified Pluto as a *dwarf planet* in 2006. There are five officially recognised dwarf planets: Pluto, Eris, Makemake, Haumea, and Ceres.
- **"The Oort cloud has been observed."** It has not — its existence is inferred from comet orbits.
- **"Our Solar System is typical."** Probably not — most exoplanet systems are quite different.

## Connections
The architecture of the Solar System is the baseline for *comparative planetology* — studying other planetary systems to learn what is generic and what is special about ours. The Kuiper belt and Oort cloud are the sources of comets that may have brought water and organics to the early Earth. Planet formation connects to *Solid State Physics* (the minerals in meteorites), *Atomic and Molecular Physics* (spectroscopy of protoplanetary disks), and *Astrophysics I* (the celestial sphere — comets are visible from Earth).

## Takeaway
- Inner planets are small and rocky; outer planets are large and gaseous.
- Asteroid belt: Mars–Jupiter; Kuiper belt: beyond Neptune; Oort cloud: outer reaches.
- The Solar System formed from a collapsing molecular cloud, $4.57$ Gyr ago.
- The nebular hypothesis explains the disk's flatness, the prograde orbits, and the composition gradient.
- The Solar System's architecture is just one of many possible outcomes of planet formation.
