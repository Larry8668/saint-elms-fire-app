# Terrestrial and Jovian Planets

## Overview
The inner Solar System is dominated by small, dense, rocky worlds with iron cores and (in three of four cases) thin atmospheres. The outer Solar System is dominated by massive gas giants with deep atmospheres, no solid surface, and (in two of four cases) internal heat sources. This lesson compares the two families, explains the differentiation that produced them, and ties their properties to mass and composition.

## Learning Path
- What you should already know: basic properties of the planets, gravitational binding, the nebular hypothesis.
- What this lesson adds: an explanation of the structural differences between rocky and gaseous worlds.
- What it unlocks: planetary science beyond the Solar System, the study of super-Earths and mini-Neptunes, and the structure of exoplanet atmospheres.

## Core Explanation
**Differentiation.** A newly formed planet is a hot, largely homogeneous mixture of rock and metal. Heavier elements (iron, nickel) sink to the centre under gravity, while lighter silicates rise to form a mantle. The process releases gravitational energy as heat, which keeps the interior molten and prolongs differentiation. Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars are all differentiated.

The Earth's structure: an inner solid iron core, an outer liquid iron core, a silicate mantle (mostly solid but with slow convection), and a thin crust. The core is about $1.2$ Earth radii in size (radius $3.5 \times 10^6\text{ m}$), the mantle extends to about $6.4 \times 10^6\text{ m}$.

**Why is Mercury so dense?** Mercury has an unusually large iron core (about $75\%$ of its radius). Theories: a giant impact stripped off much of the silicate mantle; or the solar nebula was hotter than expected near Mercury's orbit, leaving only metals to condense.

**Why is Mars small and cool?** Mars is less massive, so it cooled faster. Its core is solid, and it lacks an active magnetic field (although it had one early in its history). With a thin atmosphere and no plate tectonics, it has been a "frozen world" for billions of years.

**Venus's runaway greenhouse.** Venus's atmosphere is $96.5\%$ $\text{CO}_2$ with a surface pressure of about $92\text{ atm}$. Despite being closer to the Sun than Earth, the early Venus may have had oceans similar to Earth's. As the Sun brightened, the water vapour content of the atmosphere increased, enhancing the greenhouse effect, which raised the surface temperature, which released more $\text{CO}_2$ from rocks, and so on. The result is a surface temperature of about $740\text{ K}$ — hot enough to melt lead.

**Earth's habitable conditions.** Earth is in the *habitable zone* — the range of distances from the Sun where liquid water can exist on the surface. Combined with plate tectonics (which recycles $\text{CO}_2$ and regulates the climate), a magnetic field (which deflects the solar wind), and a large Moon (which stabilises the obliquity), Earth is uniquely hospitable among the terrestrial planets.

**Jovian planet structure.** The gas giants are mostly hydrogen and helium, with maybe a rocky/icy core of $10$–$20 M_\oplus$. Above the core, hydrogen is in *metallic* form (at pressures of millions of atmospheres, electrons are squeezed off the atoms and the hydrogen conducts electricity). Above the metallic hydrogen layer, molecular hydrogen and helium form the deep atmosphere, gradually thinning to the visible cloud tops.

**Jupiter.** Mass $317.8 M_\oplus$, radius $11.2 R_\oplus$, mean density $1.33\text{ g/cm}^3$ (less than Earth's, but $4\times$ water). Jupiter radiates about $1.7\times$ the energy it receives from the Sun, mostly leftover heat of formation. Its rapid rotation ($9.9$ hours) flattens it into an oblate spheroid and produces strong zonal winds and the visible banded structure.

**Saturn.** Mass $95.2 M_\oplus$, radius $9.45 R_\oplus$, mean density $0.687\text{ g/cm}^3$ — less than water (Saturn would float in a sufficiently large bathtub). Like Jupiter, it has a metallic hydrogen interior and a banded atmosphere. Its spectacular rings are made of water ice and lie inside the Roche limit.

**Uranus and Neptune.** The *ice giants*, mostly water, methane, and ammonia ices above a rocky core. They have significant internal heat sources (Neptune radiates $2.7\times$ the energy it receives from the Sun), strong magnetic fields that are tilted and offset from the rotation axis, and methane in their atmospheres that gives them their blue colour.

**Why gas giants at large distances?** Beyond the *frost line* (about $2.7$ AU in the early Solar System), water and other volatiles could condense into ices, providing more solid material for planet formation. A larger solid core ($\sim 10 M_\oplus$) can then accrete gas rapidly before the disk disperses.

**Why are the gas giants hydrogen-dominated?** The early Solar System's protoplanetary disk was mostly hydrogen and helium (the same composition as the Sun and the Galaxy). The gas giants captured this gas directly; the terrestrial planets could not, because they were too small and the gas had dispersed.

## Key Ideas
- Terrestrial planets are differentiated into iron cores and silicate mantles.
- Venus's atmosphere is a runaway greenhouse; Earth's climate is regulated by plate tectonics.
- Gas giants have metallic hydrogen interiors and deep molecular atmospheres.
- Ice giants (Uranus, Neptune) are mostly water/methane/ammonia above a rocky core.
- The frost line and disk dispersal explain why the gas giants are large and far out.

## Worked Examples
**Example 1 — Mean density of Saturn.** Mass $5.68 \times 10^{26}\text{ kg}$, radius $5.82 \times 10^7\text{ m}$. Volume $\tfrac{4}{3} \pi R^3 = 8.27 \times 10^{23}\text{ m}^3$. Density $\approx 687\text{ kg/m}^3 = 0.687\text{ g/cm}^3$. Less than water.

**Example 2 — Surface gravity on Mars.** $g = G M/R^2 = 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 6.4 \times 10^{23} / (3.4 \times 10^6)^2 \approx 3.7\text{ m/s}^2$. About $38\%$ of Earth's.

**Example 3 — Escape velocity from Jupiter.** $v_\text{esc} = \sqrt{2 G M/R} = \sqrt{2 \times 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 1.9 \times 10^{27} / 7.0 \times 10^7} \approx 60\text{ km/s}$. Jupiter can hold onto hydrogen easily; the terrestrial planets could not, even if they had accreted it.

## Common Misconceptions
- **"Saturn would float in water."** Theoretically yes — its mean density is less than water. But there is no bathtub large enough to test it.
- **"Jupiter is a star."** Not quite — Jupiter is $0.001 M_\odot$, and the smallest red dwarf stars are about $0.08 M_\odot$. Jupiter would need to be about $80\times$ more massive to ignite deuterium fusion.
- **"The gas giants have solid surfaces."** They don't — they transition smoothly from gas to liquid to (possibly) a rocky core deep inside. There is no surface to land on.
- **"Uranus and Neptune are twins."** They are similar in size, but Neptune is denser, has a stronger internal heat source, and has more active atmospheric features.

## Connections
Planetary structure is the meeting point of *Mechanics* (hydrostatic equilibrium of a self-gravitating sphere), *Solid State Physics* (the minerals and ices), *Atomic and Molecular Physics* (atmospheric spectroscopy), and *Nuclear Physics* (radiogenic heating in the core). Comparative planetology applies the same tools to exoplanets, and the diversity of exoplanet systems is challenging our Solar-System-centric understanding.

## Takeaway
- Terrestrial planets are differentiated: iron core, silicate mantle, thin crust.
- Venus's atmosphere is a runaway greenhouse; Earth's climate is stabilised by plate tectonics.
- Gas giants have metallic hydrogen interiors and deep molecular atmospheres.
- Ice giants (Uranus, Neptune) are mostly water, methane, and ammonia ices.
- The frost line and disk dispersal explain the radial structure of the Solar System.
