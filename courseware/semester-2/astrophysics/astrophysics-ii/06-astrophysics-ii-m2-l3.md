# Small Bodies — Asteroids, Comets, Kuiper Belt

## Overview
The Solar System is not just planets. Hundreds of thousands of asteroids, billions of comets, and a population of small icy bodies beyond Neptune make up the *small bodies* of the system. They are fossils from the formation epoch and the source of meteorites, which fall to Earth. This lesson surveys the populations, their composition, and the dynamic phenomena (tails, impacts) they produce.

## Learning Path
- What you should already know: the architecture of the Solar System, the basic difference between rocky and icy bodies.
- What this lesson adds: a structural and compositional picture of asteroids, comets, and the Kuiper belt.
- What it unlocks: meteorite science, the impact history of the Earth, and the study of protoplanetary disks.

## Core Explanation
**Asteroids.** Small rocky bodies, mostly in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter. Sizes range from $940\text{ km}$ (Ceres, now classified as a dwarf planet) down to a few metres. The total mass is about $4\%$ of the Moon's.

Asteroid composition (Tholen or Bus–DeMeo taxonomy):
- **C-type (carbonaceous)**: dark, carbon-rich, similar in composition to the Sun (minus volatiles). About $75\%$ of known asteroids.
- **S-type (silicaceous)**: stony, with metal content. About $17\%$.
- **M-type (metallic)**: mostly iron–nickel; rare.

**Near-Earth asteroids (NEAs).** Asteroids whose orbits cross or come close to Earth's. Sub-classes: Atens (semi-major axis < 1 AU), Apollos (Earth-crossing, $a > 1$ AU), Amors (approach but do not cross), Atiras (entirely inside Earth's orbit). NEAs are the focus of planetary defence efforts.

**Trojan asteroids.** Asteroids that share an orbit with a planet, librating around the L4 or L5 Lagrangian points. Jupiter has the most (over $10{,}000$ known), but Neptune, Mars, and Earth have small Trojan populations too.

**Comets.** Icy bodies, mostly in the Kuiper belt (short-period) or the Oort cloud (long-period). A comet's structure:
- **Nucleus**: a few km across, a "dirty snowball" of water ice, $\text{CO}_2$ ice, dust, and rocky grains.
- **Coma**: when the comet approaches the Sun, ice sublimates, releasing gas and dust that form a fuzzy atmosphere up to $100{,}000\text{ km}$ across.
- **Tails**: two distinct tails. The *ion tail* (gas) points directly away from the Sun, driven by the solar wind. The *dust tail* curves behind the comet's orbit, driven by radiation pressure. They can extend millions of km.
- **Hydrogen envelope**: an enormous cloud of hydrogen (up to tens of millions of km) seen in UV.

**Famous comets.** Halley's comet: period $75$–$76$ years, last perihelion 1986, next 2061. Comet Hale–Bopp (1997): one of the brightest of the 20th century. Comet Hyakutake (1996): long ion tail. 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko, visited by the Rosetta mission (2014–2016).

**Kuiper belt objects (KBOs).** Icy bodies beyond Neptune. Pluto, Eris, Makemake, Haumea, Sedna, Quaoar, and many smaller. KBOs are dynamically cold (low-eccentricity, low-inclination) or hot (high-eccentricity, high-inclination). The cold classical KBOs are thought to be the least-altered remnants of the protoplanetary disk.

**Oort cloud comets.** Long-period comets with periods of thousands to millions of years come from the Oort cloud. They are perturbed into the inner Solar System by passing stars, galactic tides, or (rarely) molecular clouds. The Oort cloud may contain trillions of comets with total mass comparable to Earth's.

**Meteoroids, meteors, meteorites.** A *meteoroid* is a small body in space. If it enters Earth's atmosphere and vaporises, it produces a *meteor* (a "shooting star"). If a piece survives and lands, it is a *meteorite*. Most meteorites are fragments of asteroids; a few percent are from Mars (ejected by large impacts) or the Moon.

**Meteor showers.** When Earth crosses the orbit of a defunct comet, the debris produces a meteor shower. The Perseids (August, from comet Swift–Tuttle) and the Leonids (November, from comet Tempel–Tuttle) are well-known. The meteors appear to radiate from a single point in the sky — the radiant.

**Meteorite types.**
- *Stony meteorites*: chondrites (with chondrules, primordial Solar System particles) and achondrites (igneous, from differentiated parent bodies).
- *Iron meteorites*: iron–nickel, from the cores of disrupted asteroids.
- *Stony–iron meteorites*: mixtures.

**The asteroid–meteorite link.** Most meteorites come from the asteroid belt. Specific meteorite classes are linked to specific asteroid classes. Some rare meteorites (carbonaceous chondrites) contain organic compounds and water — they may have delivered water and prebiotic molecules to the early Earth.

**Impacts.** The Earth has been hit many times. The Chicxulub impact ($66$ Mya) ended the age of dinosaurs. The Moon's craters record the impact history. Modern surveys (NEOWISE, Pan-STARRS) catalogue near-Earth objects; deflection strategies (kinetic impactor, gravity tractor) are being developed.

## Key Ideas
- Asteroids: rocky, mostly in the belt; C-, S-, and M-types are the most common.
- Comets: icy, with nucleus, coma, and two tails (ion, dust).
- KBOs: icy bodies beyond Neptune; Oort cloud comets come from the outer reaches.
- Meteorites are mostly asteroidal; a few percent are from Mars or the Moon.
- Meteor showers occur when Earth crosses a comet's debris trail.

## Worked Examples
**Example 1 — Cometary orbit.** Halley's comet has $a \approx 17.8$ AU, $e \approx 0.967$. Its period is $T = a^{3/2} \approx 75$ years. Perihelion distance: $q = a(1 - e) \approx 0.586$ AU, just inside Mercury's orbit. Aphelion: $Q = a(1 + e) \approx 35$ AU, near Pluto's orbit.

**Example 2 — NEA approach.** A $100$-m asteroid on an Earth-crossing orbit has impact energy $\sim \tfrac{1}{2} m v^2 \approx 5 \times 10^{15}\text{ J}$ (about $1$ Gt of TNT, enough to devastate a country). The energy is set by the kinetic energy at impact, not the size of the asteroid alone.

**Example 3 — Comet tail length.** The ion tail of a bright comet can extend up to $1$ AU. The solar wind speed is about $400\text{ km/s}$, so the time for ions to travel $1$ AU is $\sim 10^6\text{ s} \approx 12$ days. A comet at $1$ AU from the Sun has a tail that has been growing for about a week or two.

## Common Misconceptions
- **"Asteroids are densely packed."** They are not — typical separations are millions of km.
- **"A comet's tail trails behind its motion."** The ion tail points away from the Sun, not behind the orbit. As the comet moves, the tail swings around.
- **"Comets are burning."** They are not — they are sublimating. The bright head is sunlight scattered by the coma and tails.
- **"All meteorites are from asteroids."** Most are, but a small fraction are from Mars or the Moon, blasted off by impacts.

## Connections
The small bodies are the most pristine samples of the early Solar System. Meteorites are studied in the lab with the tools of *Solid State Physics* and *Atomic and Molecular Physics*; their isotopic compositions are clocks for the formation epoch. The Kuiper belt is the closest analog of the protoplanetary disk. The impact history of the Earth ties to *Nuclear Physics* (cosmic-ray exposure ages), *Geophysics* (impact craters), and the *origin of life* (delivery of water and organics).
